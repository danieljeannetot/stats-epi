Lesson 6 · Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests

Part I · Foundations

Coverage of confidence intervals, p-values, errors and power, multiple testing, and evidence of absence.

About 3 hours Before this: Lessons 4–5 Threads: uncertainty · frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • interpret a confidence interval through its coverage across repeated studies;
  • explain what a p-value is, and list what it is not;
  • describe type I and type II errors and calculate power;
  • explain why small studies that reach significance exaggerate effects;
  • control false positives when testing many hypotheses, and know when not to;
  • use an equivalence test to support “no meaningful effect”.

Why it matters. Confidence intervals and p-values are reported in almost every epidemiological paper, and misread in many of them. “Not significant” read as “no effect” has buried real harms; a single p < 0.05 from a small study has launched false alarms. The aim of this lesson is to make you a careful reader and writer of both.

6.1 Confidence intervals and coverage

Each simulated study draws a sample of children and computes a 95% confidence interval for the risk of asthma. The true risk in the whole cohort is known. A 95% interval comes from a procedure that, across many studies, captures the truth 95% of the time. Any single interval either contains it or doesn’t. Run 100 studies and count the misses.

interval contains the truthinterval missestrue risk

AStandard notation

\[\hat\pi \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)}{n}}\] \[P_\pi\big(L \le \pi \le U\big) = 1 - \alpha \ \text{ for every } \pi\]

\(z_{0.975} = 1.96\) for a 95% interval. The probability statement is about the procedure, over repeated samples.

BModel list and R

smp <- cohort |> slice_sample(n = 200)
p  <- mean(smp$asthma)
se <- sqrt(p * (1 - p) / 200)
p + c(-1.96, 1.96) * se

binom.test(sum(smp$asthma), 200)$conf.int

CHow it’s reported

WarningCommon pitfall

“There is a 95% probability that the true risk lies between 9% and 18%.” For a confidence interval, the 95% describes the method, not this interval. The statement in quotes is what a Bayesian credible interval says (Lesson 19).

Further reading: Gardner and Altman (1986) argued for reporting confidence intervals rather than p-values; Greenland et al. (2016) list common misinterpretations of intervals; Kirkwood and Sterne (2003) derive intervals for risks and rates.

6.2 Hypothesis tests and p-values

A test asks: if there were truly no effect, how surprising would data like ours be? The p-value is the probability, computed under the null hypothesis, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. To see its behavior, run many studies comparing asthma risk between two groups. First use a fake “exposure” assigned by coin toss, so the null is true; then the real exposure, high modeled NO₂.

AStandard notation

\[H_0: \pi_1 = \pi_0 \quad\text{vs}\quad H_1: \pi_1 \ne \pi_0\] \[z = \frac{\hat\pi_1 - \hat\pi_0}{\sqrt{\bar\pi(1-\bar\pi)\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_0}\right)}}, \qquad p = 2\,P(Z \ge |z|)\]

BModel list and R

smp <- cohort |> slice_sample(n = 500) |>
  mutate(high = no2_modeled >= 30)
prop.test(table(smp$high, smp$asthma)[, 2:1],
          correct = FALSE)

CHow it’s reported

Report the effect estimate with its confidence interval and the exact p-value, not only “p < 0.05”.

NoteR code (optional): p-values under the null and under a real effect 
set.seed(6)
one_study <- function(n, real = TRUE) {
  smp <- slice_sample(cohort, n = n)
  expo <- if (real) smp$no2_modeled >= 30 else runif(n) < 0.5
  prop.test(c(sum(smp$asthma[expo]), sum(smp$asthma[!expo])),
            c(sum(expo), sum(!expo)), correct = FALSE)$p.value
}
p_null <- map_dbl(1:1000, \(i) one_study(500, real = FALSE))
p_real <- map_dbl(1:1000, \(i) one_study(500, real = TRUE))
c(null_below_0.05 = mean(p_null < 0.05), real_below_0.05 = mean(p_real < 0.05))
#> null_below_0.05 real_below_0.05 
#>           0.048           0.258

Further reading: Wasserstein and Lazar (2016) is the American Statistical Association’s statement on p-values; Greenland et al. (2016) explain 25 common misinterpretations; Matthews and Altman (1996) on comparing effect sizes rather than p-values.

6.3 Errors, power and exaggerated estimates

Rejecting a true null is a type I error, with probability α. Missing a real effect is a type II error. Power is the probability of detecting an effect of a given size, and it depends on the effect, the baseline risk, the sample size and α. Small studies have low power, and there is a second, less obvious problem: when a low-powered study does reach significance, its estimate tends to be far too large.

WarningCommon pitfall

Treating a significant result from a small study as a reliable estimate of the effect. Among the studies that pass p < 0.05, the estimates are biased upward, often severely. This “winner’s curse” is one reason early findings shrink when replicated.

Further reading: Button et al. (2013) show how low power undermines reliability, including the exaggeration of significant effects; Greenland et al. (2016) on misinterpretations of power; Kirkwood and Sterne (2003) give sample size and power calculations for epidemiological comparisons.

6.4 Multiple testing

Test twenty exposures that have no effect at all, each at α = 0.05, and you expect one “significant” result by chance alone. The chance of at least one false positive is 64%. A Bonferroni correction divides α by the number of tests, at a cost in power. Whether to correct depends on the question: confirming a single pre-specified hypothesis, or exploring many.

AStandard notation

\[P(\text{at least one false positive}) = 1 - (1-\alpha)^m\] \[\text{Bonferroni: reject } H_{0j} \text{ if } p_j < \alpha / m\]

BModel list and R

p_values <- c(0.003, 0.02, 0.04, 0.30)
p.adjust(p_values, method = "bonferroni")
p.adjust(p_values, method = "holm")

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Bender and Lange (2001) explain when and how to adjust for multiple testing; Rothman (1990) argues that adjustment is often unnecessary when results are interpreted with care; Greenland et al. (2016) place both views in context.

6.5 Absence of evidence and equivalence

A non-significant result means the data are compatible with no effect. They may also be compatible with a large effect. “Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.” To support the claim that an effect is negligible, set a margin in advance, here a risk ratio between 0.9 and 1.1, and check whether the confidence interval lies entirely inside it. Move the estimate and its precision and watch the four possible conclusions.

TipBayesian preview

A frequentist test can reject “no effect” but can never support it; that needs the separate equivalence test above. A Bayesian analysis can state the probability that the effect lies inside the margin (a “region of practical equivalence”), and a Bayes factor can weigh “no effect” against “some effect” directly. Lesson 23 compares the approaches.

Further reading: Altman and Bland (1995) on why a non-significant result is not evidence of no effect; Lakens (2017) is a practical primer on equivalence tests (TOST); Greenland et al. (2016) on interpreting non-significant results.

Check your understanding

Key takeaways

  • A 95% confidence interval comes from a procedure that captures the truth in 95% of studies. Its width shows precision.
  • A p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme if the null were true. It is not the probability that the null is true, nor a measure of effect size.
  • Under a true null, p-values are uniformly distributed: 5% fall below 0.05 by chance.
  • Power depends on effect size, baseline risk, sample size and α. Significant results from low-powered studies exaggerate effects.
  • Testing many hypotheses inflates false positives; correct when confirming, interpret carefully when exploring.
  • Non-significant does not mean no effect. To support “no meaningful effect”, use an equivalence margin.
