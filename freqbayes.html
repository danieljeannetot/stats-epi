Frequentist and Bayesian, side by side

Part V · Bayesian modeling

Two meanings of probability, what each kind of interval and probability claims, when the two approaches agree, six situations where they disagree, coverage, and how to choose and report.

About 3 hours Before this: Parts I–IV Threads: frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian · uncertainty · shrinkage Purpose: every purpose; see the map

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • state what a confidence interval, a credible interval, a p-value and a posterior probability each claim;
  • recognize the conditions under which frequentist and Bayesian analyses give nearly the same numbers;
  • explain six situations where they genuinely disagree, and which answer is more useful in each;
  • check the calibration of intervals by simulation, in the frequentist and the Bayesian sense;
  • choose an approach for a given study, and report it so readers of either school can follow.

Why it matters. Parts I–IV used mostly frequentist tools; Part V is Bayesian. Students often leave such courses believing either that the two are interchangeable, or that they are opposed camps. Both are wrong. They usually agree on the numbers and differ in what the numbers mean; and in a handful of important situations they disagree on the numbers too. Knowing which is which is what lets you use both well.

FB.1 Two meanings of probability

Frequentist probability describes how often something happens over repeated samples: it belongs to procedures. The true risk is a fixed unknown number; the data are random. Bayesian probability describes how strongly a statement is supported given what is known: it belongs to propositions. The data are fixed once observed; uncertainty about the risk is expressed as a distribution. The same study then supports different kinds of statements:

Frequentist Bayesian
Interval 95% confidence interval: produced by a method that captures the true value in 95% of repeated studies 95% credible interval: given the model, prior and data, the true value lies in it with probability 0.95
Evidence against “no effect” p-value: probability of data at least this extreme if there were no effect Posterior probability of an effect, P(β > 0 | data); or a Bayes factor comparing hypotheses
Estimate Maximum likelihood estimate, with a sampling distribution Posterior distribution, summarized by median or mean
Prior knowledge Enters through design and model choice, not formally Enters formally through the prior
What is random The data (and so the interval) The unknown parameter, in the sense of uncertainty about it
Calibrated when Coverage holds for every possible true value Coverage holds averaged over the prior

The most common misreading in published papers is reading a confidence interval as a credible interval: “there is a 95% probability that the true OR lies between 1.02 and 1.30”. Strictly, only the Bayesian interval licenses that sentence, and only given its prior.

Further reading: Bland and Altman (1998) contrast the two in a page; Efron (1986) asks why not everyone is Bayesian; Greenland (2006) introduces the Bayesian perspective for epidemiologists.

FB.2 When they agree

Estimate the risk of asthma from a sample of children, both ways. With enough data and a prior that isn’t strongly informative, the confidence and credible intervals almost coincide: the likelihood dominates the posterior, and both summarize the same likelihood. Shrink the sample, or choose an informative or misleading prior, and they part.

Further reading: Bayarri and Berger (2004) on why the two approaches often coincide and when they don’t; Brown et al. (2001) on binomial confidence intervals, including those with Bayesian justifications.

FB.4 Calibration: is each interval honest?

A frequentist 95% interval promises 95% coverage for every true value. A Bayesian 95% interval promises 95% coverage on average over the prior: if the true values really came from the prior. Simulate 2,000 studies and count. Fix the true risk far from the prior’s center and the credible interval falls short; draw true risks from the prior and it hits 95%, while the confidence interval may be needlessly wide or, for small samples, miss.

TipCalibrated Bayes

A practical synthesis used by many applied statisticians: fit Bayesian models, because they handle complex structures and prior knowledge naturally, and check them with frequentist tools: coverage in simulations, posterior predictive checks, and out-of-sample prediction (Little 2006). Lessons 22–25 work in this spirit.

Further reading: Little (2006) on calibrated Bayes; Gelman et al. (2013) on frequency properties of Bayesian procedures.

FB.5 Choosing and reporting

Situation Often the more convenient choice Why
Large data, simple model, regulatory or conventional reporting Frequentist Fast, familiar, few choices to defend
Small samples, rare events, separation Bayesian with weakly informative priors (or penalized likelihood) Finite, stable estimates; honest uncertainty
Hierarchies with few groups (districts, schools) Bayesian Uncertainty about variance parameters propagates
Many related estimates Bayesian partial pooling Shrinkage controls noise without blanket multiplicity corrections
Real prior evidence (earlier studies) Bayesian Uses it explicitly and transparently
Complex derived quantities (MMT, attributable numbers) Either, by simulation; Bayesian draws are the most direct Uncertainty propagates through any function
Pre-registered confirmatory test with error-rate guarantees Frequentist Error rates are the design target

Whatever the choice, report so that both kinds of reader can follow: the estimate and its interval, what the interval means, the prior (and a sensitivity analysis) for Bayesian analyses, and the model checks. When both analyses are cheap, reporting both, and noting where they differ, is often the most informative choice.

Further reading: Kruschke (2021) on reporting Bayesian analyses; Wasserstein and Lazar (2016) on reporting p-values; Spiegelhalter et al. (2004) on Bayesian methods in health research.

Key takeaways

  • Frequentist probability belongs to procedures over repeated samples; Bayesian probability to statements given the data and prior.
  • With enough data and weakly informative priors, the two give nearly the same numbers, with different interpretations.
  • They disagree with few events, separation, variances near zero, many related estimates, data-dependent stopping, and real prior information.
  • Confidence intervals aim at coverage for every true value; credible intervals at coverage averaged over the prior. Simulation shows both.
  • Choose by the problem, not by camp; report so both kinds of reader can follow.

Concepts at a glance

Ideas from this lesson that are easily confused, side by side.

Concept What it is How it differs
Confidence interval From a procedure capturing the truth in 95% of repeated samples. A statement about the procedure; a credible interval is about the parameter.
Credible interval Contains the parameter with probability 0.95, given the prior and data. Depends on the prior; calibrated on average over the prior.
p-value vs posterior probability P(data this extreme null) vs P(hypothesis
Likelihood principle All evidence from the data is in the likelihood. Bayesian inference follows it; frequentist error rates depend on stopping rules too.
Shrinkage vs Bonferroni Pulling noisy estimates toward a common mean vs widening every interval. Two answers to multiplicity: modeling vs penalizing.
Separation An exposure level where all, or none, have the outcome. Makes the ML estimate infinite; a weak prior or penalization fixes it.
