Lesson 20 · Priors: what they say and how much they matter

Part V · Bayesian modeling

Prior predictive checks, why vague priors aren’t neutral, eliciting priors from subject knowledge, prior sensitivity, and priors as penalties that shrink and smooth.

About 3 hours Before this: Lesson 19 Threads: shrinkage · frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • check what a prior implies about the data with a prior predictive simulation;
  • explain why “vague” priors are often unreasonable on the scale of the data;
  • translate subject-matter knowledge into a prior distribution;
  • judge how much a conclusion depends on the prior, and how that changes with the amount of data;
  • explain a prior as a penalty, and use it to shrink or smooth a set of related coefficients.

Why it matters. The prior is the part of a Bayesian analysis readers question most. Defending it requires knowing what it actually says, which is rarely obvious from its formula. A prior that looks harmless on the coefficient scale can claim that a hot day might bring a million emergency visits. Conversely, a well-chosen prior is one of the most useful tools in this course: it is how shrinkage and smoothing, introduced in Lessons 11, 17 and 18, enter a Bayesian model.

20.1 What does a prior say? Prior predictive checks

A prior on α and β in log μ = α + β(T − 20) is hard to judge directly. Simulate from it instead: draw (α, β) from the prior, compute the curve of expected visits against temperature, and look. That is a prior predictive check. “Vague” priors with large standard deviations, often presented as letting the data speak, imply absurd numbers of visits.

AStandard notation

\[\alpha^{(s)} \sim p(\alpha), \quad \beta^{(s)} \sim p(\beta), \quad \tilde\mu^{(s)}(T) = e^{\alpha^{(s)} + \beta^{(s)}(T - 20)}\] \[\tilde y^{(s)} \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\big(\tilde\mu^{(s)}(T)\big)\]

BModel list and R

a <- rnorm(100, log(8), 0.5)
b <- rnorm(100, 0, 0.05)
exp(a + b * (35 - 20))     # visits at 35°C

brms::brm(..., sample_prior = "only")
NoteR code (optional): a prior predictive check 
set.seed(20)
prior_draws <- function(sd_a, sd_b, n = 1000)
  tibble(a = rnorm(n, log(8), sd_a), b = rnorm(n, 0, sd_b)) |>
  mutate(visits_35 = exp(a + b * 15))

bind_rows(vague = prior_draws(10, 1), weak = prior_draws(0.5, 0.05), .id = "prior") |>
  group_by(prior) |>
  summarise(median_visits_35C = median(visits_35),
            share_above_1000 = mean(visits_35 > 1000))
#> # A tibble: 2 × 3
#>   prior median_visits_35C share_above_1000
#>   <chr>             <dbl>            <dbl>
#> 1 vague              3.34            0.381
#> 2 weak               8.17            0

Further reading: Gabry et al. (2019) on prior predictive checks in the Bayesian workflow; Gelman et al. (2017) on understanding priors through the likelihood and data they imply; McElreath (2020) on simulating from priors.

20.2 Eliciting a prior from subject knowledge

Experts rarely think in log rate ratios, but they can usually say what range of effects they find plausible: “heat probably raises visits among older people by somewhere between 0% and 4% per degree.” That statement translates directly into a normal prior on β. Set the range, and check what the prior implies for a hot day.

AStandard notation

\[\beta \sim \mathrm{Normal}(m, s^2), \quad m = \frac{\log(1 + L) + \log(1 + U)}{2}, \quad s = \frac{\log(1 + U) - \log(1 + L)}{2 \times 1.96}\]

\(L\) and \(U\): the lowest and highest plausible effects, read as a 95% prior range.

BModel list and R

L <- 0.00; U <- 0.04
m <- (log1p(L) + log1p(U)) / 2
s <- (log1p(U) - log1p(L)) / (2 * 1.96)
prior(normal(m, s), class = b)   # brms

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Spiegelhalter et al. (2004) on eliciting priors from clinical and epidemiological experts; Greenland (2006) on priors expressed as plausible ranges of relative risks; Greenland (2007) on informative priors in regression.

20.3 How much does the prior matter?

The same heat model from Lesson 19 is fitted under four priors, for increasing amounts of data. With little data, the priors give different answers. As data accumulate, the posteriors converge, unless the prior is so strong that it effectively ignores them. Reporting results under several priors is a sensitivity analysis.

WarningCommon pitfall

Choosing the prior after seeing which one gives the preferred answer. Like choosing a spline’s flexibility by its result (Lesson 11), this invalidates the analysis. Specify the prior in advance, justify it, and report sensitivity to reasonable alternatives.

Further reading: Gelman et al. (2013) on sensitivity analysis; Spiegelhalter et al. (2004) on skeptical and enthusiastic priors as a community of opinions; Greenland (2006) on presenting results under several priors.

20.4 Priors as penalties: shrinking and smoothing

The posterior mode under a normal prior is the same as a penalized maximum likelihood estimate: the prior adds a penalty for coefficients far from its mean. This is the bridge between Bayesian priors and the penalized splines of Lesson 11. Here it is applied to the unconstrained distributed lag model of Lesson 16. An independent prior shrinks each lag coefficient toward zero; a random-walk prior shrinks neighboring lags toward each other, smoothing the curve.

AStandard notation

\[\text{independent: } \beta_\ell \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0, 1/\lambda)\] \[\text{random walk: } \beta_\ell - \beta_{\ell-1} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0, 1/\lambda)\] \[\log p(\beta \mid y) = \ell(\beta) - \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,\beta^\top P \beta + \text{const}\]

\(P = I\) for the independent prior; \(P = D^\top D\), with \(D\) the first-difference matrix, for the random walk.

BModel list and R

b[1] ~ normal(0, 0.1)
b[l] ~ normal(b[l - 1], sigma_rw)    # l = 2..L
# mgcv: the same penalty, chosen by REML
mgcv::gam(visits ~ s(lag_matrix, by = temp_matrix,
                     bs = "ps"), ...)
TipBayesian preview

Here λ is fixed by hand. In a hierarchical model it becomes a parameter with its own prior, and the data decide how much to shrink or smooth, just as τ was estimated in Lesson 18. Lesson 24 makes this the central idea.

Further reading: Gelman et al. (2008) propose weakly informative default priors that act as gentle penalties; Eilers and Marx (1996) on difference penalties, the random-walk prior in frequentist form; Wood (2017) on the link between penalties and priors.

Key takeaways

  • Judge a prior by what it implies about data: simulate from it.
  • “Vague” priors are not neutral; on the scale of the data they can be absurd, and they can distort comparisons between models.
  • Plausible ranges from subject knowledge translate directly into normal priors on log relative risks.
  • The prior’s influence fades as data accumulate; report sensitivity to reasonable alternatives.
  • A normal prior acts as a penalty. Independent priors shrink toward zero; random-walk priors smooth.
