Study design: before the method

Part I · Foundations

Design and analysis are separate choices. What each design compares and controls, the same question answered by four designs, matching designs to purposes, and why no method rescues a weak design.

About 1.5 hours Before this: Lesson 1 Threads: bias · uncertainty Purpose: every purpose; see the map

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • distinguish a study’s design from the statistical method used to analyze it;
  • say what a design controls before any model is fitted, and what it leaves to the analysis;
  • explain why a case-control study can estimate an odds ratio but not a risk, and why it is efficient;
  • recognize an ecological design and its limits;
  • match designs to research purposes, and explain why no method rescues a design that can’t answer the question.

Why it matters. Most of this course is about methods: models, estimators, priors. But the most important decisions in a study are made before any data are analyzed: who is compared with whom, how exposure is measured, and when. A clever method applied to a weak design gives a precise answer to the wrong question. “For objective causal inference, design trumps analysis” (Rubin 2008).

D.1 Two separate choices

A study design decides which units are compared, how exposure is assigned or observed, and when outcomes and exposures are measured. A method extracts an estimate, with its uncertainty, from the data that design produced. They are chosen in that order.

The design decides The method decides
Who is compared Exposed vs unexposed people, cases vs controls, days vs days, a person vs their own other days How the comparison is summarized: a risk ratio, an odds ratio, a curve
Confounding What is controlled by design: randomization, self-matching, restriction, time-series structure What is adjusted for by modeling, but only among variables that were measured
What can be estimated Risks or only odds ratios; individual or area-level effects The functional form, the uncertainty, the reference value
Precision How many units, of which kind, are measured How efficiently the information is used

The two choices are partly independent. The same method serves different designs: Poisson regression is used in cohorts, in daily time series and in ecological studies, and answers a different question in each. And one design can be analyzed by different methods: a case-crossover study by conditional logistic or conditional Poisson regression (Lesson 15), a cohort by g-computation or inverse probability weighting (Lesson 12), frequentist or Bayesian.

D.2 One question, four designs

Does NO₂ raise the risk of childhood asthma? Here the same 5,000 children are used to mimic four designs, each answering with its own data: the full cohort; a case-control study, taking every child with asthma and a random sample of children without; a random sample of the same size as the case-control study; and an ecological study of the 12 districts’ asthma rates and mean NO₂. The analysis is the same kind of logistic model each time, adjusted for SES and green space, and uses the true NO₂ so that design is the only thing that differs. The true odds ratio is 1.15 per 10 µg/m³.

AStandard notation

\[\text{case-control: } \mathrm{logit}\, P(Y = 1 \mid x, \text{sampled}) = \alpha^{*} + \beta x, \qquad \alpha^{*} = \alpha + \log\frac{s_1}{s_0}\]

\(s_1\), \(s_0\): sampling fractions of cases and controls. Sampling by outcome shifts only the intercept, so the odds ratio \(e^{\beta}\) is unchanged, but the risk is not estimable without the sampling fractions.

BHow to read it

  • \(\alpha^{*}\): the intercept in the case-control sample: shifted by the sampling.
  • \(\beta\): the log odds ratio: unchanged by sampling on the outcome.
  • \(s_1\): the sampling fraction of cases; \(s_0\) of controls.

CModel list and R

cases    <- filter(cohort, asthma == 1)
controls <- filter(cohort, asthma == 0) |>
  slice_sample(n = 2 * nrow(cases))
cc <- bind_rows(cases, controls)
glm(asthma ~ I(no2_true / 10) + ses + green,
    family = binomial, data = cc)

Further reading: Lash et al. (2021) on cohort and case-control designs; Greenland and Morgenstern (1989) and Robinson (1950) on ecological designs.

D.3 What each design controls

Each design removes some sources of bias by construction and leaves others to the analysis. Designs marked “not covered” are not taught in this course but are important in environmental epidemiology.

Further reading: Maclure (1991) on the case-crossover design; Gasparrini (2021) on the case time series design; Petersen et al. (2016) on self-controlled case series; Bernal et al. (2017) on interrupted time series for evaluating interventions; Hernán and Robins (2016) on emulating a target trial.

D.5 No method rescues a weak design

Some failures belong to the design and can’t be undone by analysis:

  • Comparing the wrong units. An ecological association between district NO₂ and district asthma rates can’t, by itself, say how a child’s own exposure affects that child (Lesson 17).
  • Selecting on a consequence. Studying only children seen at a respiratory clinic conditions on a collider and creates bias that no adjustment removes (Lesson 9).
  • Missing confounders. Adjustment works only for what was measured; an E-value can say how strong unmeasured confounding would need to be, not remove it (Lesson 12).
  • Measuring exposure badly. Correcting for measurement error requires validation data, which must be designed into the study from the start (Lessons 13 and 24).
  • Too little information. A study too small to detect the effect of interest is uninformative whatever the method (Lesson 6).

Good design also includes designing the data: a validation subsample, enough clusters rather than just enough people (Lesson 18), exposure histories at the resolution the lags require (Lesson 16), and a plan written before the data are seen.

Key takeaways

  • Design decides who is compared and what is controlled before any model is fitted; method extracts the estimate from those data.
  • The same method can serve different designs, and the same design can be analyzed by different methods.
  • Case-control studies estimate odds ratios efficiently, but not risks; ecological studies compare areas, not people.
  • Every design suits some purposes and not others: check the match before collecting data.
  • No method rescues a design that can’t answer the question.

Concepts at a glance

Ideas from this lesson that are easily confused, side by side.

Concept What it is How it differs
Design Who is compared, how exposure arises, and when things are measured. Decided before data; controls some biases by construction.
Method How an estimate is extracted from the data. Can only adjust for what the design measured.
Cohort People followed from exposure to outcome. Estimates risks; a case-control study only odds ratios.
Case-control Cases and a sample of non-cases, compared on exposure. Efficient for rare outcomes; risks not estimable without sampling fractions.
Cross-sectional Exposure and outcome measured at the same time. Measures prevalence; can’t establish which came first.
Ecological Areas or groups compared, not individuals. Associations between areas need not hold for individuals (ecological fallacy).
Controlled by design vs by modeling Removed by how data are collected vs adjusted for in analysis. Design can remove unmeasured confounders (e.g., self-matching); modeling can’t.
