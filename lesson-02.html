Lesson 2 · Probability and conditional probability

Part I · Foundations

The rules of probability, conditional probability and independence, Bayes’ theorem, and diagnostic tests.

About 2 hours Before this: Lesson 1 Threads: frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian (first appearance of Bayes’ rule)

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • compute joint, marginal and conditional probabilities from a 2×2 table;
  • decide whether two events are independent, and explain what that means for a study;
  • use Bayes’ theorem to turn sensitivity, specificity and prevalence into predictive values;
  • explain why a good test can still produce mostly false positives;
  • update a probability with a likelihood ratio, the simplest form of Bayesian reasoning.

Why it matters. “Children whose parents smoke are more likely to develop asthma” is a statement about conditional probability. So is “a positive wheeze questionnaire means a child probably has asthma”, and that one is often wrong. Reversing a conditional probability is one of the most common reasoning errors in medicine, and the same error reappears later as the misreading of p-values.

2.1 Probability and its rules

A probability is a number between 0 and 1. In a cohort, the probability of an event is estimated by the proportion of children who experience it. Two events can be combined: both happen (the intersection, written A ∩ B), or at least one happens (the union, A ∪ B). Choose two events in the asthma cohort. The mosaic plot divides the children into the four combinations, with each area proportional to the number of children.

Event A is always "the child develops asthma by age 8".

asthma (A)no asthma

AStandard notation

\[P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\] \[P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}\] \[\text{independent} \iff P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)\]

BModel list and R

cohort |>
  summarise(
    p_A       = mean(asthma),
    p_B       = mean(parent_smoke),
    p_AandB   = mean(asthma & parent_smoke),
    p_AorB    = mean(asthma | parent_smoke),
    p_A_givB  = mean(asthma[parent_smoke]),
    p_A_givnB = mean(asthma[!parent_smoke]))

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Blitzstein and Hwang (2019) develop probability from first principles; Kirkwood and Sterne (2003) introduce it for medical statistics; Clayton and Hills (1993) connect probability to epidemiological models.

2.2 Conditional probability and independence

Conditioning restricts attention to the children for whom one event is true. In the mosaic plot, P(A | B) is the height of the orange part in the left column, and P(A | not B) in the right column. If the two heights are equal, knowing B tells you nothing about A: the events are independent. If they differ, the events are associated. Association between an exposure and an outcome is exactly what epidemiology measures; the risk ratio from Lesson 1 is P(A | B) / P(A | not B).

The order of conditioning matters. P(asthma | positive questionnaire) and P(positive questionnaire | asthma) are different numbers, and the next section shows how far apart they can be. Switch event B to the questionnaire and compare them.

NoteR code (optional): joint and conditional probabilities 
cohort |>
  count(parent_smoke, asthma) |>
  mutate(p_joint = n / sum(n)) |>
  group_by(parent_smoke) |>
  mutate(p_asthma_given_smoke = n / sum(n)) |>
  ungroup()
#> # A tibble: 4 × 5
#>   parent_smoke asthma     n p_joint p_asthma_given_smoke
#>   <lgl>         <dbl> <int>   <dbl>                <dbl>
#> 1 FALSE             0  3385  0.677                 0.879
#> 2 FALSE             1   465  0.093                 0.121
#> 3 TRUE              0   929  0.186                 0.808
#> 4 TRUE              1   221  0.0442                0.192
# independence would make these two equal
with(cohort, c(p_A = mean(asthma),
               p_A_given_B = mean(asthma[parent_smoke])))
#>         p_A p_A_given_B 
#>   0.1372000   0.1921739

Further reading: Blitzstein and Hwang (2019) on conditioning and independence; Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) show that natural frequencies make conditional reasoning much easier; Lash et al. (2021) on measures of association as contrasts of conditional risks.

2.3 Bayes’ theorem and diagnostic tests

The wheeze questionnaire is used to screen for asthma. Its sensitivity is the probability of a positive result in a child with asthma; its specificity the probability of a negative result in a child without. Parents and doctors want the reverse: given a positive result, how likely is asthma? That is the positive predictive value (PPV), and Bayes’ theorem gives it. The icon array shows 1,000 children; change the prevalence, sensitivity and specificity.

true positivefalse negativefalse positivetrue negative

AStandard notation

\[Se = P(T^+ \mid D^+) \qquad Sp = P(T^- \mid D^-)\] \[PPV = P(D^+ \mid T^+) = \frac{Se \cdot p}{Se \cdot p + (1 - Sp)(1 - p)}\]

\(p\) = prevalence, \(D^+\) = has asthma, \(T^+\) = positive test.

BModel list and R

cohort |>
  summarise(
    sens = mean(wheeze_q[asthma == 1]),
    spec = mean(!wheeze_q[asthma == 0]),
    ppv  = mean(asthma[wheeze_q]),
    npv  = mean(1 - asthma[!wheeze_q]))

CHow it’s reported

WarningCommon pitfall

Reading sensitivity as the probability that a positive child has the condition. With a prevalence of 1%, a test with 90% sensitivity and 90% specificity gives a PPV of only about 8%: more than nine in ten positives are false. Try the “rare condition” button.

NoteR code (optional): diagnostic accuracy in the cohort 
cohort |>
  count(wheeze_q, asthma) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = asthma, values_from = n, names_prefix = "asthma_")
#> # A tibble: 2 × 3
#>   wheeze_q asthma_0 asthma_1
#>   <lgl>       <int>    <int>
#> 1 FALSE        3900      122
#> 2 TRUE          414      564
cohort |>
  summarise(sens = mean(wheeze_q[asthma == 1]),
            spec = mean(!wheeze_q[asthma == 0]),
            ppv  = mean(asthma[wheeze_q]),
            npv  = mean(1 - asthma[!wheeze_q]))
#> # A tibble: 1 × 4
#>    sens  spec   ppv   npv
#>   <dbl> <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
#> 1 0.822 0.904 0.577 0.970

Further reading: Altman and Bland (1994a) and Altman and Bland (1994b) are short classics on sensitivity, specificity and predictive values; Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) and Hoffrage et al. (2000) show how natural frequencies, like the tree above, prevent errors.

2.4 Likelihood ratios: updating a probability

Bayes’ theorem has an especially simple form on the odds scale. A likelihood ratio says how many times more likely a test result is in children with asthma than without. Multiply the pre-test odds by it, and you get the post-test odds. This is updating: start from what you believed before the test, and let the evidence move you. The same logic, applied to parameters instead of diagnoses, is the core of Bayesian statistics (Part V).

after a positive resultafter a negative resultno test

AStandard notation

\[LR^+ = \frac{Se}{1 - Sp} \qquad LR^- = \frac{1 - Se}{Sp}\] \[\text{post-test odds} = \text{pre-test odds} \times LR\]

BModel list and R

lr_pos    <- sens / (1 - spec)
pre_odds  <- p_pre / (1 - p_pre)
post_odds <- pre_odds * lr_pos
p_post    <- post_odds / (1 + post_odds)

CHow it’s reported

TipBayesian preview

Replace “has asthma” with “the true effect of NO₂ is β” and “test result” with “the data”. Bayes’ theorem then turns a prior belief about β and the likelihood of the data into a posterior belief: \(p(\beta \mid \text{data}) \propto p(\text{data} \mid \beta)\, p(\beta)\). The pre-test probability becomes the prior, the likelihood ratio becomes the likelihood, and the post-test probability becomes the posterior. Lesson 19 builds this up step by step.

Further reading: Deeks and Altman (2004) explain likelihood ratios for test results; Bland and Altman (1998) contrast Bayesian and frequentist reasoning in a page; Held and Sabanés Bové (2020) develop likelihood and Bayesian inference together.

Key takeaways

  • Probabilities of combined events follow simple rules: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).
  • P(A | B) restricts attention to cases where B is true. P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different.
  • Events are independent when conditioning on one doesn’t change the probability of the other. An exposure–outcome association is a departure from independence.
  • Bayes’ theorem reverses a conditional probability. Predictive values depend strongly on prevalence.
  • On the odds scale, updating is a multiplication by the likelihood ratio, the simplest form of Bayesian reasoning.
