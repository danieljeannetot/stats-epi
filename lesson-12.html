Lesson 12 · Causal inference: from association to effect

Part III · Causal inference, measurement and disease burden

Potential outcomes, identification assumptions, g-computation, propensity scores and inverse probability weighting, positivity, and sensitivity to unmeasured confounding.

About 3.5 hours Before this: Lessons 8–9 Threads: bias · uncertainty · frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • state a causal question as a contrast between potential outcomes, under a well-defined intervention;
  • explain exchangeability, positivity and consistency, and why each is needed;
  • estimate a marginal causal effect by g-computation, and explain why it can differ from an adjusted odds ratio;
  • estimate the same effect by inverse probability weighting, check balance and diagnose positivity problems;
  • quantify how strong unmeasured confounding would need to be to explain away an estimate.

Why it matters. Policy questions are causal: how many cases of childhood asthma would a 10 µg/m³ reduction in NO₂ prevent? An adjusted odds ratio from Lesson 9 doesn’t answer that directly. Causal inference makes the question precise, says which assumptions the answer depends on, and provides estimators that target the answer itself.

12.1 Potential outcomes: two worlds, one observed

Imagine two worlds. In one, every child lives with the NO₂ they actually had. In the other, an intervention has reduced every child’s NO₂ by Δ µg/m³. Each child has a potential outcome in each world. The causal effect compares the two worlds for the same children. Real data show only the first world. Here, because the data are simulated, both can be shown.

asthma in both worldsasthma prevented by the reductionno asthma in either world

AStandard notation

\[Y_i^{x}: \text{ outcome of child } i \text{ if exposure were set to } x\] \[RR_{\text{causal}} = \frac{E[Y^{X-\Delta}]}{E[Y^{X}]}, \qquad RD_{\text{causal}} = E[Y^{X-\Delta}] - E[Y^{X}]\]

Only \(Y_i^{X_i}\), the outcome under the exposure actually received, is ever observed (consistency).

BModel list and R

# the causal question, written as a contrast:
# E[asthma | everyone's NO2 lowered by 10]
#   vs E[asthma | NO2 as observed]
# Identification needs:
#   exchangeability given (SES, green)
#   positivity: every child could have had
#     NO2 10 µg/m3 lower
#   consistency: "lowering NO2" is well defined

Further reading: Hernán and Robins (2020) develop causal inference from potential outcomes; Hernán and Robins (2016) on framing observational analyses as emulations of a target trial; Greenland et al. (1999) on the formal definition of confounding.

12.2 G-computation: estimating the two worlds from data

G-computation, also called standardization, estimates each world with an outcome model. Fit a regression of asthma on NO₂ and confounders; predict every child’s risk with their own NO₂, and again with NO₂ lowered by Δ; average each set of predictions; compare. The result is a marginal effect for this population, the quantity the policy question asks for, and it can be compared directly with the truth from 12.1.

AStandard notation

\[\widehat{E}[Y^{x}] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \widehat{P}(Y = 1 \mid X = x_i,\ Z = z_i)\]

Evaluated once with each child’s own \(x_i\), once with \(x_i - \Delta\); \(Z\) = confounders.

BModel list and R

fit <- glm(asthma ~ no2_true + ses + green,
           family = binomial, data = cohort)
r1 <- mean(predict(fit, cohort, type = "response"))
r0 <- mean(predict(fit,
        mutate(cohort, no2_true = no2_true - 10),
        type = "response"))
c(rr = r0 / r1, rd = r0 - r1)

CHow it’s reported

NoteR code (optional): g-computation with a bootstrap confidence interval 
gcomp <- function(d, delta = 10) {
  fit <- glm(asthma ~ no2_true + ses + green, family = binomial, data = d)
  r_obs <- mean(predict(fit, d, type = "response"))
  r_int <- mean(predict(fit, mutate(d, no2_true = no2_true - delta), type = "response"))
  c(rr = r_int / r_obs, cases_prevented_per_1000 = 1000 * (r_obs - r_int))
}
gcomp(cohort)
#>                       rr cases_prevented_per_1000 
#>                0.8880198               15.3636874
set.seed(12)
boot <- map(1:200, \(b) as_tibble_row(gcomp(slice_sample(cohort, prop = 1, replace = TRUE)))) |>
  bind_rows()
apply(boot, 2, quantile, c(0.025, 0.975))
#>              rr cases_prevented_per_1000
#> 2.5%  0.7689192                0.4975408
#> 97.5% 0.9963491               31.5739105
WarningCommon pitfall

Reading an adjusted odds ratio as a population effect. The aOR is conditional on the covariates, and because odds ratios are non-collapsible it differs from the marginal OR even without confounding. G-computation turns a model into the marginal risk ratio or risk difference the question asks for.

Further reading: Snowden et al. (2011) demonstrate g-computation step by step; Hernán and Robins (2020) treat standardization and the g-formula; Keiding and Clayton (2014) link it to classical standardization.

12.3 Propensity scores and inverse probability weighting

A second route models the exposure instead of the outcome. The propensity score is each child’s probability of high NO₂ (here ≥ 30 µg/m³) given the confounders. Weighting each child by the inverse probability of the exposure they actually had creates a pseudo-population in which the confounders are balanced between exposed and unexposed. Check the overlap and the balance, and see what happens if the propensity model includes a variable that predicts exposure strongly but isn’t a confounder.

Propensity model for high NO₂ (≥ 30 µg/m³):

high NO₂lower NO₂

AStandard notation

\[e(z) = P(A = 1 \mid Z = z), \qquad w_i = \frac{A_i}{e(z_i)} + \frac{1 - A_i}{1 - e(z_i)}\] \[\widehat{E}[Y^{a}] = \frac{\sum_i w_i\, 1\{A_i = a\}\, Y_i}{\sum_i w_i\, 1\{A_i = a\}}\]

BModel list and R

d <- cohort |> mutate(high = no2_true >= 30)
ps <- glm(high ~ ses + green, family = binomial,
          data = d) |> fitted()
d$w <- ifelse(d$high, 1 / ps, 1 / (1 - ps))
with(d, weighted.mean(asthma[high], w[high]) /
        weighted.mean(asthma[!high], w[!high]))

CHow it’s reported

NoteR code (optional): weights, balance and the weighted risk ratio 
d <- cohort |> mutate(high = no2_true >= 30)
ps_fit <- glm(high ~ ses + green, family = binomial, data = d)
d <- d |> mutate(ps = fitted(ps_fit), w = if_else(high, 1 / ps, 1 / (1 - ps)))

smd <- function(x, g, w) {
  m1 <- weighted.mean(x[g], w[g]); m0 <- weighted.mean(x[!g], w[!g])
  (m1 - m0) / sqrt((var(x[g]) + var(x[!g])) / 2)
}
tibble(variable = c("ses", "green"),
       before = c(smd(d$ses, d$high, rep(1, nrow(d))), smd(d$green, d$high, rep(1, nrow(d)))),
       after  = c(smd(d$ses, d$high, d$w), smd(d$green, d$high, d$w)))
#> # A tibble: 2 × 3
#>   variable before   after

12.4 How strong would unmeasured confounding have to be?

Every observational estimate assumes no unmeasured confounding. The E-value asks: how strongly would an unmeasured confounder need to be associated with both the exposure and the outcome (as risk ratios) to fully explain away the observed association? Here, pretend green space had not been measured, and see how the estimate and its E-value compare.

AStandard notation

\[E = RR + \sqrt{RR\,(RR - 1)}\] \[\text{explains away if } \frac{RR_{EU}\, RR_{UD}}{RR_{EU} + RR_{UD} - 1} \ge RR\]

\(RR_{EU}\): confounder–exposure association; \(RR_{UD}\): confounder–outcome association.

BModel list and R

e_value <- function(rr) rr + sqrt(rr * (rr - 1))
e_value(1.12)   # point estimate
e_value(1.01)   # lower confidence limit
# or: EValue::evalues.RR(1.12, lo = 1.01)

CHow it’s reported

TipBayesian preview

In a Bayesian analysis, g-computation is done on every posterior draw of the outcome model, giving a full posterior for the causal risk ratio. Sensitivity to unmeasured confounding can be expressed as a prior on the bias parameters, so that uncertainty about the confounder enters the final interval (probabilistic bias analysis, Lesson 13).

Further reading: VanderWeele and Ding (2017) introduced the E-value; Fox et al. (2021) on quantitative bias analysis more generally; Hernán and Robins (2020) on the role of each identification assumption.

Key takeaways

  • A causal effect compares potential outcomes for the same population under two well-defined interventions. Only one is ever observed.
  • Identifying it from data needs exchangeability (no unmeasured confounding), positivity and consistency.
  • G-computation predicts both worlds from an outcome model and averages, giving marginal risk ratios and risk differences.
  • Inverse probability weighting balances confounders by weighting; check balance, overlap and extreme weights.
  • Don’t put non-confounders that strongly predict exposure into a propensity model: they worsen positivity and precision.
  • E-values express how strong unmeasured confounding would need to be to explain an association away.
