Lesson 25 · Capstone: a district-level Bayesian DLNM with spatial pooling

Part V · Bayesian modeling

A complete analysis from question to report: district-specific distributed lag non-linear models, Bayesian pooling with a BYM2 spatial prior, heat- and cold-attributable visits on every posterior draw, sensitivity analysis and reporting.

About 5 hours Before this: the whole course, especially Lessons 14, 16, 17, 18 and 24 Threads: all five

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • plan a multi-location temperature–health analysis, and choose between one-stage and two-stage designs;
  • fit a distributed lag non-linear model in each district, and summarize it by its cumulative exposure–response curve;
  • pool the district curves with a Bayesian hierarchical model whose prior lets neighboring districts borrow strength from each other;
  • compute minimum morbidity temperatures, attributable visits and exceedance probabilities on every posterior draw;
  • check, stress-test and report the analysis.

Why it matters. A city health department wants to know which districts suffer most from heat, so that cooling centers, outreach and warnings go where they matter. Each district’s own data are too few for a reliable answer; the city-wide answer ignores real differences. This lesson brings the course together to answer the question properly: the DLNM of Lesson 16, the pooling of Lessons 18 and 24, the spatial structure of Lesson 17, and the burden calculations of Lesson 14, with uncertainty carried through every step.

25.1 The question, the data and the design

The question. For each of the city’s 12 districts: how do daily emergency visits depend on temperature, accumulated over three weeks of lags; at what temperature is risk lowest; and how many visits each year are attributable to heat and to cold?

The design. A two-stage analysis, the standard in multi-location studies (Gasparrini et al. 2012; Sera and Gasparrini 2022). Stage 1 fits a DLNM in each district separately. Stage 2 pools the districts’ cumulative curves with a Bayesian hierarchical model. A one-stage model, fitting everything at once, is possible in Stan or INLA but much slower; with daily counts in hundreds per district, the two-stage approximation is accurate.

Two versions of the data. In the simulated city, all districts share the same underlying sensitivity to heat; only their temperatures and age structures differ. That is a legitimate finding, but it makes the case for spatial modeling hard to see. So the lesson also offers a vulnerability scenario. The heat sensitivity there is increased overall and varies between districts, rising with the urban heat island and with deprivation, so that central and deprived districts are much more sensitive. The data are regenerated in the browser; the course data files are unchanged.

Further reading: Gasparrini et al. (2015) is a landmark multi-location study using this design; Sera and Gasparrini (2022) review extensions of two-stage designs; Bhaskaran et al. (2013) on the time-series foundations.

25.2 Stage 1: a DLNM in each district

Each district gets the model of Lesson 16: quasi-Poisson regression of daily visits on a cross-basis of its own temperature (a natural spline in temperature, three degrees of freedom, with knots common to all districts, crossed with a natural spline in lag over 0–21 days), a spline of time with 7 degrees of freedom per year, and day of week. The fitted surface is then reduced to its cumulative curve: three coefficients per district, with their 3 × 3 covariance matrix. These 12 small summaries are all that stage 2 needs.

stage 1 estimate, 95% CItruth

AStandard notation

\[\log\mu_{jt} = \alpha_j + \sum_{k,q}\eta_{jkq}\, w_{jt,kq} + s_j(t) + \gamma_j^\top \mathrm{dow}_t\] \[\hat\theta_{jk} = \sum_q \hat\eta_{jkq}\sum_{\ell=0}^{21} c_q(\ell), \qquad \log RR_j(x) = \sum_k \hat\theta_{jk}\,\big[b_k(x) - b_k(x_{\text{ref}})\big]\]

\(\hat\theta_j\): district \(j\)’s three cumulative coefficients, with covariance \(S_j\).

BModel list and R

cb <- dlnm::crossbasis(d$temp, lag = 21,
  argvar = list(fun = "ns", knots = kn),
  arglag = list(fun = "ns",
                knots = dlnm::logknots(21, 3)))
fit <- glm(visits ~ cb + splines::ns(t, 35) + dow,
           family = quasipoisson, data = d)
red <- dlnm::crossreduce(cb, fit)   # overall
coef(red); vcov(red)
NoteR code (optional): stage 1 for all districts with dlnm 
dd <- visits |>
  group_by(district_id, district, date) |>

  group_by(district_id) |>

kn <- quantile(dd$temp, c(0.05, 0.35, 0.65, 0.95))    # knots common to all districts

stage1 <- dd |>
  group_by(district_id, district) |>
  group_modify(\(d, key) {
    cb <- dlnm::crossbasis(d$temp, lag = 21,
            argvar = list(fun = "ns", knots = kn[2:3], Boundary.knots = kn[c(1, 4)]),
            arglag = list(fun = "ns", knots = dlnm::logknots(21, 3)))
    fit <- glm(visits ~ cb + splines::ns(t, df = 35) + dow, family = quasipoisson, data = d)
    red <- dlnm::crossreduce(cb, fit)
    tibble(coef = list(coef(red)), vcov = list(vcov(red)))
  }) |>
  ungroup()

Further reading: Gasparrini (2011) on crossreduce(); Gasparrini et al. (2012) on reducing DLNMs before pooling; Gasparrini et al. (2010) on the cross-basis.

25.3 Stage 2: Bayesian pooling with a spatial prior

Stage 2 treats each district’s coefficients as a noisy measurement of its true coefficients, θ̂_j ~ N(θ_j, S_j), and models the true ones as a city-wide mean plus a district deviation. The BYM2 prior splits each deviation into an unstructured part (any district can differ) and a spatially structured part (neighbors tend to differ in the same way), with a mixing parameter φ, the share of variation that is spatial, estimated from the data. Compare three choices: no pooling (stage 1 as it is), exchangeable pooling (φ = 0, the Bayesian version of Lesson 18), and BYM2.

posterior median, 95% CrItruth

AStandard notation

\[\hat\theta_j \sim \mathrm{N}(\theta_j, S_j), \qquad \theta_{jk} = \mu_k + \sigma_k\big(\sqrt{1-\phi}\, v_{jk} + \sqrt{\phi}\, u_{jk}\big)\] \[v_{jk} \sim \mathrm{N}(0, 1), \qquad u_{\cdot k} \sim \text{ICAR (scaled)}, \qquad \sigma_k \sim \text{Half-N}(0, 0.3^2), \quad \phi \sim \mathrm{U}(0, 1)\]

ICAR: each district’s \(u\) is centered on the average of its neighbors’. Scaling makes \(\sigma_k\) comparable whatever the map.

BModel list and R

# INLA, one coefficient at a time (sketch)
f <- theta_hat ~ 1 + f(district, model = "bym2",
                       graph = g, scale.model = TRUE)
INLA::inla(f, data = s1k, family = "gaussian",
  control.family = list(hyper = list(prec =
    list(initial = log(1 / se2), fixed = TRUE))))

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Besag et al. (1991) introduced the BYM model; Riebler et al. (2016) the BYM2 parameterization used here; Morris et al. (2019) implement it in Stan; Simpson et al. (2017) on penalized complexity priors for φ and σ.

25.4 Burden on every posterior draw

Every posterior draw gives a complete set of district curves, so every derived quantity gets a posterior too: each district’s minimum morbidity temperature, its heat- and cold-attributable visits, and the probability that its heat burden exceeds a policy threshold. That last quantity, an exceedance probability, is often what decision-makers need: not “what is the burden?” but “how sure are we that it is high here?”.

AStandard notation

\[\mathrm{AN}_j^{(s)} = \sum_{t:\, T_{jt} > \mathrm{MMT}_j^{(s)}} y_{jt}\Big(1 - e^{-\{f_j^{(s)}(T_{jt}) - f_j^{(s)}(\mathrm{MMT}_j^{(s)})\}}\Big)\] \[P\big(\mathrm{AN}_j / \mathrm{pop}_j > c \mid y\big) \approx \frac{1}{S}\sum_s 1\{\mathrm{AN}_j^{(s)} / \mathrm{pop}_j > c\}\]

BModel list and R

# for each draw s and district j:
f   <- B %*% theta[s, j, ]           # curve
mmt <- grid[which.min(f)]
an  <- sum(y_j[temp_j > mmt] *
           (1 - exp(-(f_t - f_mmt))))
# then summarise over draws:
mean(an / pop_j * 1e5 / years > 60)

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Gasparrini and Leone (2014) on attributable numbers from DLNMs; Gasparrini et al. (2015) on heat and cold burden across locations; Rockhill et al. (1998) on interpreting attributable fractions.

25.5 Checking, sensitivity and reporting

Before reporting, three checks. Sensitivity to the prior: with only 12 districts, the prior on the between-district SDs matters (Lesson 24). Comparison with simpler analyses: does the pooled answer agree with Lesson 16’s city-wide curve and Lesson 18’s two-stage estimates? Comparison with the truth, available only because the data are simulated: did pooling bring the district curves closer to it?

Reporting the analysis. A complete report states the question, the two-stage design and every modeling choice (bases, knots, lags, time control), the stage-2 model and its priors, the computation (sampler, iterations, convergence checks), the results with credible intervals and exceedance probabilities, and the sensitivity analyses. TipExtensions

A random-walk prior on the long-term trend, instead of a spline of time, lets the data choose its smoothness (Lesson 20): in INLA, f(week, model = "rw2"); in brms, s(t, bs = "ps"). A one-stage model fits all districts’ daily counts at once, with the BYM2 prior on the cross-basis coefficients; it avoids the stage-2 normal approximation but takes hours rather than seconds. Effect modifiers such as deprivation can enter stage 2 as covariates (meta-regression), separating explained from unexplained spatial variation.

The five threads, one last time.

Thread Where it appears in this analysis
Likelihood Each district’s DLNM is a likelihood for its coefficients; stage 2 uses its normal approximation.
Uncertainty Every curve, MMT and attributable number carries a posterior interval, propagated from stage 1 through stage 2.
Shrinkage District curves are pulled toward the city mean and toward their neighbors, in proportion to how imprecise they are.
Bias Time control by splines guards against seasonal confounding; temperature is measured at district level, not personally (Lesson 13).
Frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian Stage 1 is a frequentist GLM; stage 2 is Bayesian; the combination is standard practice and can be compared with fully frequentist two-stage meta-analysis.

Further reading: Kruschke (2021) on reporting Bayesian analyses; Gelman et al. (2020) on the full Bayesian workflow; Riebler et al. (2016) on prior choices for BYM2.

Check your understanding

Key takeaways

  • Multi-location temperature studies usually use two stages: a DLNM per location, reduced to its cumulative curve, then pooling.
  • Pooling stabilizes district curves; a BYM2 prior lets districts borrow from neighbors as well as from the city, in proportions estimated from the data.
  • With few districts, spatial pooling may add little to exchangeable pooling; the data decide, and the result should be reported either way.
  • Every derived quantity, including MMTs, attributable visits and exceedance probabilities, is computed on each posterior draw.
  • Check prior sensitivity, compare with simpler analyses, and report every modeling choice.
