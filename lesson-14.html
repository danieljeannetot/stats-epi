Lesson 14 · Measuring disease burden

Part III · Causal inference, measurement and disease burden

Population attributable fractions, attributable cases, the counterfactual exposure, DALYs, and propagating uncertainty into burden estimates.

About 3 hours Before this: Lessons 1, 12 and 13 Threads: uncertainty · bias · frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • compute a population attributable fraction for a binary exposure, and know which formula is valid under confounding;
  • compute the burden of a continuous exposure relative to a stated counterfactual level;
  • combine years of life lost and years lived with disability into DALYs;
  • propagate uncertainty in relative risks into an interval for attributable cases;
  • explain why attributable fractions for different risk factors can add up to more than 100%.

Why it matters. “How many cases of childhood asthma in our city are due to traffic pollution?” is the question policy makers ask, and the answer depends on more than the relative risk. It depends on how many children are exposed, on what level of NO₂ counts as “no excess” (zero? the WHO guideline?), and on the uncertainty in every input. Burden estimates also inherit every bias from Lessons 9, 12 and 13.

14.1 Attributable fractions for a binary exposure

The population attributable fraction (PAF) is the share of cases in a population that would not occur without the exposure. It combines how common the exposure is with how much it raises risk. Levin’s formula uses the exposure prevalence and the risk ratio; it is valid only with an unconfounded risk ratio. With confounding, Miettinen’s formula, which uses the proportion of cases exposed and an adjusted risk ratio, remains valid.

AStandard notation

\[PAF_{\text{Levin}} = \frac{p_e\,(RR - 1)}{1 + p_e\,(RR - 1)}\] \[PAF_{\text{Miettinen}} = p_c\,\frac{RR_{\text{adj}} - 1}{RR_{\text{adj}}}\]

\(p_e\) = proportion of the population exposed; \(p_c\) = proportion of cases exposed.

BModel list and R

d  <- cohort |> mutate(high = no2_true >= 30)
pe <- mean(d$high)
pc <- mean(d$high[d$asthma == 1])
paf_levin <- pe * (rr - 1) / (1 + pe * (rr - 1))
paf_miet  <- pc * (rr_adj - 1) / rr_adj

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Levin (1953) proposed the attributable fraction; Miettinen (1974) gave the formula valid under confounding; Rockhill et al. (1998) review the use and misuse of PAFs; Greenland and Robins (1988) on what attributable fractions do and don’t measure.

14.2 Continuous exposures and the counterfactual level

NO₂ isn’t present or absent: every child is exposed to some level. The burden is computed against a counterfactual exposure, often called the theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL). Children above it contribute excess risk; children at or below it contribute none. The choice of counterfactual can matter more than any statistical decision. Move it, and compare zero, the WHO 2021 guideline of 10 µg/m³, and the former guideline of 40.

AStandard notation

\[PAF = 1 - \frac{E\big[\,P(Y = 1 \mid X = \min(x_i, x^*), Z = z_i)\,\big]}{E\big[\,P(Y = 1 \mid X = x_i, Z = z_i)\,\big]}\] \[AN = PAF \times D\]

\(x^*\) = counterfactual exposure; \(D\) = total cases. This is g-computation (Lesson 12) with the intervention “cap NO₂ at \(x^*\)”.

BModel list and R

fit <- glm(asthma ~ no2_true + ses + green,
           family = binomial, data = cohort)
r_obs <- mean(predict(fit, type = "response"))
r_cf  <- mean(predict(fit,
  mutate(cohort, no2_true = pmin(no2_true, 10)),
  type = "response"))
paf <- 1 - r_cf / r_obs

CHow it’s reported

WarningCommon pitfall

Reporting a burden estimate without stating the counterfactual exposure, or comparing burden estimates that used different ones. A PAF relative to zero NO₂ and a PAF relative to 40 µg/m³ answer different questions, and can differ several-fold.

NoteR code (optional): PAF and attributable cases for several counterfactuals 
fit <- glm(asthma ~ no2_true + ses + green, family = binomial, data = cohort)
r_obs <- mean(predict(fit, type = "response"))
paf_at <- function(x_star) {
  r_cf <- mean(predict(fit, mutate(cohort, no2_true = pmin(no2_true, x_star)), type = "response"))
  1 - r_cf / r_obs
}
tibble(counterfactual = c(0, 10, 20, 40)) |>
  mutate(paf = map_dbl(counterfactual, paf_at),
         cases_per_10000 = 10000 * r_obs * paf)
#> # A tibble: 4 × 3
#>   counterfactual     paf cases_per_10000
#>            <dbl>   <dbl>           <dbl>
#> 1              0 0.306             420. 
#> 2             10 0.215             295. 
#> 3             20 0.122             168. 
#> 4             40 0.00803            11.0

Further reading: Achakulwisut et al. (2019) estimate the global burden of paediatric asthma attributable to NO₂; Khreis et al. (2017) meta-analyze traffic pollution and childhood asthma incidence; World Health Organization (2021) set the current NO₂ guideline; Ezzati et al. (2002) describe the comparative risk assessment framework.

14.3 From cases to DALYs

Attributable cases count events; disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) measure lost healthy life, so that burdens from different diseases can be compared. DALYs add years of life lost (YLL) to early death and years lived with disability (YLD), weighted by the severity of the condition. For childhood asthma, YLD dominate. The inputs below are illustrative; real analyses take disability weights and durations from sources such as the Global Burden of Disease study.

AStandard notation

\[DALY = YLL + YLD\] \[YLL = \sum_a D_a\,L_a, \qquad YLD = I \times DW \times L_{\text{dur}}\]

Incidence-based YLD: new cases \(I\), disability weight \(DW\), average duration \(L_{\text{dur}}\).

BModel list and R

yld  <- cases * dw * duration
yll  <- deaths * life_expectancy
daly <- yll + yld

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Murray (1994) set out the technical basis of DALYs; GBD 2019 Risk Factors Collaborators (2020) report risk-factor burdens with current methods; Ezzati et al. (2002) on combining burden across risk factors.

14.4 Propagating uncertainty

A burden estimate inherits uncertainty from every input: the relative risk, the exposure distribution, the baseline risk. Plugging the confidence limits of the relative risk into the formula gives a crude range. Monte Carlo simulation does better: draw each input from a distribution expressing its uncertainty, compute the burden for each draw, and summarize the resulting distribution.



TipBayesian preview

Monte Carlo propagation is the frequentist analyst borrowing a Bayesian idea: treating inputs as uncertain quantities with distributions. In a fully Bayesian analysis, the burden is a derived quantity computed on every posterior draw, so all uncertainties, and their correlations, flow through automatically. The capstone (Lesson 25) computes heat-attributable visits and DALYs this way.

Further reading: GBD 2019 Risk Factors Collaborators (2020) describe uncertainty intervals from repeated draws; Achakulwisut et al. (2019) propagate uncertainty in exposure–response and incidence; Greenland and Robins (1988) on why attributable fractions for several risk factors needn’t sum to 100%.

Check your understanding

Key takeaways

  • The PAF combines exposure prevalence and risk ratio. Levin’s formula needs an unconfounded RR; Miettinen’s formula and g-computation work under confounding.
  • For continuous exposures, burden is defined against a counterfactual level, which must be stated and justified.
  • Attributable cases = PAF × total cases. DALYs add years of life lost and years lived with disability.
  • Burden estimates inherit uncertainty and bias from every input: propagate uncertainty by simulation and correct the relative risk for confounding and measurement error first.
  • PAFs for different risk factors can sum to more than 100%, because cases can have several causes.
