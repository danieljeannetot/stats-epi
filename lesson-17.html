Lesson 17 · Spatial data and spatial autocorrelation

Part IV · Time, space and clustering

Standardized morbidity ratios and the small-numbers problem, funnel plots, spatial autocorrelation and Moran’s I, ecological bias, and smoothing area estimates.

About 3 hours Before this: Lessons 1, 8 and 10 Threads: shrinkage · bias

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • compute standardized morbidity ratios by indirect standardization, and map them;
  • explain why the most extreme values on a map are often just the smallest areas, and use a funnel plot;
  • measure spatial autocorrelation with Moran’s I and test it by permutation;
  • explain the ecological fallacy, and why area-level associations can differ from individual-level ones;
  • describe how smoothing toward an overall or local mean makes small-area maps more reliable.

Why it matters. Maps of disease rates drive decisions about where to act. Yet a map of raw rates for small areas is mostly a map of noise, and the districts that look worst are often simply the ones with the fewest people. Neighboring districts also resemble each other, which breaks the independence assumption of standard models. This lesson shows the problems; Lesson 25 brings the Bayesian solution.

17.1 Standardized morbidity ratios and the small-numbers problem

An SMR compares the number of visits a district actually had with the number expected if each age group had the city-wide rate (indirect standardization, Lesson 1). An SMR of 1.2 means 20% more visits than expected for its age structure. Shorten the period, and the counts, and watch how noisy the map becomes. The funnel plot shows why: the range of SMRs expected by chance alone is much wider for small expected counts.

AStandard notation

\[E_j = \sum_k r_k\, PT_{jk}, \qquad SMR_j = \frac{O_j}{E_j}\] \[O_j \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(E_j\,\theta_j) \ \Rightarrow\ \mathrm{SE}(SMR_j) \approx \frac{\sqrt{O_j}}{E_j}\]

\(r_k\) = city-wide rate in age group \(k\); \(\theta_j\) = the district’s true relative risk.

BModel list and R

rates <- visits |> group_by(age_group) |>
  summarise(r = sum(visits) / sum(population))
visits |> left_join(rates, by = "age_group") |>
  group_by(district) |>
  summarise(O = sum(visits),
            E = sum(r * population),
            smr = O / E)

CHow it’s reported

WarningCommon pitfall

Ranking areas by their raw SMRs, or labeling the most extreme as “hot spots”, without considering their expected counts. On a funnel plot, a small district outside the 95% limits may still be within the range expected by chance once you allow for 12 comparisons.

Further reading: Spiegelhalter (2005) introduced funnel plots for comparing institutions; Elliott and Wartenberg (2004) review spatial epidemiology; Waller and Gotway (2004) on SMRs and indirect standardization for small areas.

17.2 Spatial autocorrelation and Moran’s I

Neighboring districts share air, housing and populations, so their values tend to be similar. Moran’s I measures this: it is positive when neighbors resemble each other, near zero when they don’t. Its significance is tested by permutation: shuffle the values among districts many times, and see how often chance alone gives an I as large as the observed one.

AStandard notation

\[I = \frac{n}{\sum_{ij} w_{ij}}\;\frac{\sum_i\sum_j w_{ij}(x_i - \bar x)(x_j - \bar x)}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2}\]

\(w_{ij} = 1\) if districts \(i\) and \(j\) are neighbors (here: share a border or a corner), row-standardized. Under no autocorrelation, \(E[I] = -1/(n-1)\).

BModel list and R

nb <- lapply(strsplit(districts$neighbors, " "),
             as.integer)
class(nb) <- "nb"
lw <- spdep::nb2listw(nb, style = "W")
spdep::moran.mc(districts$deprivation, lw,
                nsim = 999)
NoteR code (optional): SMRs and Moran’s I with spdep 
rates <- visits |> group_by(age_group) |> summarise(r = sum(visits) / sum(population), .groups = "drop")
smr <- visits |>
  left_join(rates, by = "age_group") |>
  group_by(district_id) |>
  summarise(O = sum(visits), E = sum(r * population), .groups = "drop") |>
  mutate(smr = O / E) |>
  arrange(district_id)

nb <- lapply(strsplit(districts$neighbors, " "), as.integer)
class(nb) <- "nb"
lw <- spdep::nb2listw(nb, style = "W")

17.3 Ecological associations and the ecological fallacy

With district-level data only, the association between NO₂ and asthma has to be estimated from 12 points: district asthma risk against district mean NO₂. This ecological association need not equal the individual-level one. District-level confounding, by deprivation for example, can distort it, and with few areas it is very imprecise. Concluding from an area-level association to individuals is the ecological fallacy.

Further reading: Robinson (1950) first described the ecological fallacy; Greenland and Morgenstern (1989) explain ecological bias, confounding and effect modification; Elliott and Wartenberg (2004) on when ecological designs are informative.

17.4 Smoothing small-area estimates

With small counts, each district’s SMR is noisy. An empirical Bayes estimate pulls each SMR toward the city-wide value by an amount that depends on its own precision: districts with few expected visits move a lot, large ones hardly at all. Here the five-year SMRs serve as the “truth” against which one week’s raw and smoothed SMRs are judged.

AStandard notation

\[\hat\theta_j^{\text{EB}} = w_j\, SMR_j + (1 - w_j)\,\hat\mu, \qquad w_j = \frac{\hat\sigma^2_\theta}{\hat\sigma^2_\theta + \hat\mu / E_j}\]

\(\hat\mu\) and \(\hat\sigma^2_\theta\): mean and between-district variance of the true relative risks, estimated by the method of moments (Marshall, 1991).

BModel list and R

mu   <- sum(O) / sum(E)
s2   <- sum(E * (smr - mu)^2) / sum(E) -
        mu / mean(E)
s2   <- max(s2, 0)
w    <- s2 / (s2 + mu / E)
smr_eb <- w * smr + (1 - w) * mu

CHow it’s reported

TipBayesian preview

Empirical Bayes shrinks every district toward one city-wide value. A full Bayesian disease-mapping model (BYM2, Lesson 25) shrinks each district partly toward the city mean and partly toward its neighbors, with the balance between the two estimated from the data, and with uncertainty for every district.

Further reading: Clayton and Kaldor (1987) introduced empirical Bayes estimates of relative risk for disease mapping; Marshall (1991) gave the method-of-moments estimator used here; Efron and Morris (1977) explain why shrinkage improves many estimates at once.

Key takeaways

  • SMRs compare observed with expected counts, allowing for age structure. Their precision depends on the expected count.
  • Maps of raw SMRs for small areas or short periods mostly show noise; funnel plots put each area’s value in context.
  • Neighboring areas tend to be similar. Moran’s I measures this, and permutation tests it.
  • Ecological associations can differ from individual-level ones because of area-level confounding and aggregation.
  • Shrinking noisy area estimates toward a common value, or toward neighbors, gives more accurate maps.
