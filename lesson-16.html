Lesson 16 · Distributed lag and distributed lag non-linear models

Part IV · Time, space and clustering

Lagged effects and harvesting, distributed lag models, the cross-basis, exposure–lag–response surfaces, cumulative exposure–response curves, the minimum morbidity temperature, and attributable fractions.

About 3.5 hours Before this: Lessons 11 and 15 Threads: uncertainty · shrinkage · frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • explain why exposure effects can be spread over several days, and recognize harvesting;
  • fit a distributed lag model and explain why constraining the lag curve stabilizes it;
  • describe a DLNM as a cross-basis: a spline in exposure crossed with a spline in lag;
  • read an exposure–lag–response surface, its slices, and the cumulative exposure–response curve;
  • estimate the minimum morbidity temperature with its uncertainty, and heat- and cold-attributable fractions.

Why it matters. Heat acts within a day or two; cold acts over a week or more; and part of a heat effect may be visits brought forward by a few days. A model that uses only the same day’s temperature underestimates cold effects and can exaggerate heat. DLNMs are now the standard approach in temperature–health research, including in large multi-country studies.

16.1 Lagged effects and the distributed lag model

A hot day can raise visits on the same day, the next day and the day after. A distributed lag model includes the exposure on each of the previous L days, each with its own coefficient. Here the exposure is “degrees above 20°C” (heat only). Unconstrained, the lag coefficients are noisy, because temperatures on neighboring days are highly correlated. Constraining the lag curve to a smooth shape stabilizes it. Watch for negative coefficients a few days after a hot spell: that is harvesting.

AStandard notation

\[\log\mu_t = \alpha + \sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\beta_\ell\, h_{t-\ell} + s(t) + \gamma^\top \mathrm{dow}_t, \qquad h_t = \max(0, T_t - 20)\] \[\text{constrained: } \beta_\ell = \sum_k \theta_k\, c_k(\ell), \qquad \text{cumulative} = \sum_\ell \beta_\ell\]

BModel list and R

cb <- dlnm::crossbasis(pmax(city_daily$temp - 20, 0),
        lag = 10,
        argvar = list(fun = "lin"),
        arglag = list(fun = "ns", df = 4))
fit <- glm(visits ~ cb + splines::ns(t, df = 35) + dow,
           family = quasipoisson, data = city_daily)

Further reading: Schwartz (2000) on distributed lag models and harvesting; Armstrong (2006) on lagged temperature effects; Gasparrini et al. (2010) on constraining lag structures.

16.2 The DLNM: an exposure–lag–response surface

Temperature effects are both nonlinear (a U shape) and lagged. A DLNM handles both at once with a cross-basis: a spline in temperature multiplied by a spline in lag. The result is a surface: the relative risk at every combination of temperature and lag, relative to a reference temperature. Read it through slices: pick a temperature to see how its effect unfolds over the following days, or pick a lag to see the exposure–response at that delay.

lower risk than at the referencehigher risk

AStandard notation

\[\log\mu_t = \alpha + \sum_{j}\sum_{k}\eta_{jk}\, w_{t,jk} + s(t) + \gamma^\top \mathrm{dow}_t\] \[w_{t,jk} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{L} b_j(T_{t-\ell})\, c_k(\ell)\]

\(b_j\): spline basis in temperature; \(c_k\): spline basis in lag; the \(\eta_{jk}\) define the whole surface.

BModel list and R

cb <- dlnm::crossbasis(city_daily$temp, lag = 21,
  argvar = list(fun = "ns", knots = quantile(
    city_daily$temp, c(.10, .50, .90))),
  arglag = list(fun = "ns",
    knots = dlnm::logknots(21, 3)))
fit <- glm(visits ~ cb + splines::ns(t, df = 35) +
             dow, family = quasipoisson,
           data = city_daily)

Further reading: Gasparrini et al. (2010) introduced DLNMs; Gasparrini (2011) describe the dlnm package; Bhaskaran et al. (2013) place DLNMs in the time-series framework.

16.3 The cumulative curve and the minimum morbidity temperature

Summing the surface over all lags gives the cumulative exposure–response curve: the total effect of a day’s temperature over the following three weeks. Its lowest point is the minimum morbidity temperature (MMT), the usual reference. The MMT is itself an estimate, and often an uncertain one. Simulating from the fitted coefficients shows how uncertain, and what that uncertainty does to the relative risks reported against it.

AStandard notation

\[\log RR_{\text{cum}}(x) = \sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\sum_{j,k}\eta_{jk}\,\big[b_j(x) - b_j(x_{\text{ref}})\big]\, c_k(\ell)\] \[\mathrm{MMT} = \arg\min_x \log RR_{\text{cum}}(x)\]

BModel list and R

pred <- dlnm::crosspred(cb, fit, cen = 20,
                        by = 0.1)
mmt <- pred$predvar[which.min(pred$allRRfit)]
pred2 <- dlnm::crosspred(cb, fit, cen = mmt,
                         at = quantile(city_daily$temp,
                                       c(.01, .99)))
pred2$allRRfit; pred2$allRRlow; pred2$allRRhigh

CHow it’s reported

WarningCommon pitfall

Reporting relative risks against the MMT as if the MMT were known. Conventional intervals shrink to zero width at the reference, hiding its uncertainty. Report an interval for the MMT itself, obtained by simulation or bootstrap.

NoteR code (optional): a DLNM for daily visits 
city_daily <- visits |>
  group_by(date) |>
  summarise(visits = sum(visits), temp = first(temp_city), dow = first(dow), .groups = "drop") |>
  mutate(t = row_number())

cb <- dlnm::crossbasis(city_daily$temp, lag = 21,
        argvar = list(fun = "ns", knots = quantile(city_daily$temp, c(.10, .50, .90))),
        arglag = list(fun = "ns", knots = dlnm::logknots(21, 3)))
fit <- glm(visits ~ cb + splines::ns(t, df = 35) + dow, family = quasipoisson, data = city_daily)

pred <- dlnm::crosspred(cb, fit, cen = 20, by = 0.1)
mmt <- pred$predvar[which.min(pred$allRRfit)]
pred <- dlnm::crosspred(cb, fit, cen = mmt, by = 0.1)
plot(pred, "overall", xlab = "Temperature (°C)", ylab = "Cumulative RR (lags 0-21)")

#> [1] 19.4

Further reading: Tobías et al. (2017) on estimating uncertainty in the minimum mortality temperature; Gasparrini et al. (2015) report cumulative temperature–mortality curves in 384 locations; Gasparrini (2011) on predicting from a DLNM.

16.4 Heat- and cold-attributable visits

From the cumulative curve, each day’s visits can be split into those attributable to that day’s temperature (relative to the MMT) and the rest. Summed over the period, this gives the attributable fraction for heat (days warmer than the MMT) and cold (days colder). Uncertainty is again obtained by simulating from the coefficients.

AStandard notation

\[AF_t = 1 - \exp\{-\log RR_{\text{cum}}(T_t)\}, \qquad AN = \sum_t y_t\, AF_t\] \[AF_{\text{heat}} = \frac{\sum_{t: T_t > \mathrm{MMT}} y_t\, AF_t}{\sum_t y_t}\]

TipBayesian preview

A Bayesian DLNM gives the lag curve a smoothing prior instead of a fixed number of spline knots, and computes the MMT and attributable fractions on every posterior draw, so their uncertainty comes for free. Lesson 25 builds a district-level Bayesian DLNM in which each district’s curve borrows strength from its neighbors.

Further reading: Gasparrini and Leone (2014) define attributable risk from distributed lag models; Gasparrini et al. (2015) apply it across countries; Rockhill et al. (1998) on interpreting attributable fractions.

Key takeaways

  • Exposure effects can be delayed and spread over days. Heat acts quickly; cold acts over a week or more.
  • Unconstrained lag coefficients are unstable; constraining the lag curve with a spline stabilizes them.
  • A DLNM crosses a spline in exposure with a spline in lag, giving an exposure–lag–response surface.
  • The cumulative curve sums effects over lags; its minimum, the MMT, is the usual reference, and it is uncertain.
  • Attributable fractions for heat and cold follow from the cumulative curve, with uncertainty from simulation.
