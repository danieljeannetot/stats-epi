Lesson 10 · Model checking, comparison and selection

Part II · Frequentist regression

Building and checking a model step by step, likelihood ratio tests and AIC, overfitting and cross-validation, influence, and why stepwise selection misleads.

About 2.5 hours Before this: Lessons 6 and 8 Threads: uncertainty · frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • use residuals, autocorrelation and dispersion to judge whether a model is adequate;
  • compare nested models with a likelihood ratio test, and any models with AIC or QAIC;
  • explain overfitting and estimate out-of-sample error with cross-validation;
  • identify influential observations with leverage and Cook’s distance;
  • explain why data-driven variable selection produces misleading p-values.

Why it matters. A model can fit the data you have while describing the world badly. Leftover seasonal patterns can masquerade as temperature effects; a single extreme day can move a slope; and choosing variables by their p-values guarantees that some “findings” are noise. Checking and comparing models carefully is what separates an analysis from a fishing trip.

10.1 Building and checking a model step by step

Here is the daily count of emergency visits in the whole city, modeled with Poisson regression. Add terms one at a time and watch three diagnostics: the residuals over time (patterns mean something is missing), their autocorrelation (today’s residual predicting tomorrow’s), and the dispersion. The table compares each term’s contribution with a likelihood ratio test.

Terms in the model of daily city-wide visits:

AStandard notation

\[LR = 2\,(\ell_1 - \ell_0) \sim \chi^2_{k} \ \text{under } H_0\] \[AIC = -2\ell + 2p, \qquad QAIC = -\frac{2\ell}{\hat\phi} + 2p\]

\(k\) = parameters added; \(p\) = parameters in the model. For quasi-Poisson models, the LR statistic is divided by \(\hat\phi\).

BModel list and R

m0 <- glm(visits ~ dow, family = quasipoisson,
          data = city_daily)
m1 <- update(m0, . ~ . + sin(2*pi*doy/365.25) +
               cos(2*pi*doy/365.25))
anova(m0, m1, test = "F")   # quasi-Poisson
acf(residuals(m1, type = "pearson"))

10.2 Overfitting and cross-validation

A more flexible model always fits the data it was estimated on better. The question is whether it predicts new data better. Here a polynomial in temperature is fitted to a small training sample of days, and its predictions are scored on all the other days. Increase the degree: the training error keeps falling, the test error eventually rises. That gap is overfitting. Cross-validation estimates the test error using only the training data.

training error5-fold cross-validationerror on new days

Further reading: Hastie et al. (2009) on the bias–variance trade-off and cross-validation; Harrell (2015) on overfitting and validation; Burnham and Anderson (2002) on information criteria as estimates of predictive performance.

10.3 Influential observations

Least squares squares the residuals, so a single unusual point can pull a line strongly. Influence combines an unusual outcome (a large residual) with an unusual predictor value (high leverage). Move the extra child around and watch the slope and Cook’s distance, which measures how much the fitted values change when that point is removed.

Further reading: Fox (2016) on leverage, residuals and Cook’s distance; Harrell (2015) on influential observations; Kirkwood and Sterne (2003) on outliers in medical data.

10.4 Why stepwise selection misleads

Choosing variables because they are “significant” in the same data used to report them makes those p-values meaningless. Here, 20 candidate predictors of asthma are pure noise. A screening rule keeps those with p < 0.05. Run it many times: noise variables are selected regularly, and every selected one comes with a “significant” p-value, by construction.

WarningCommon pitfall

Selecting confounders by stepwise regression or by “a change in estimate of more than 10%”, then reporting the final model’s confidence intervals as if it had been chosen in advance. Confounders should be chosen from causal knowledge (a DAG, Lesson 9), and the model fixed before looking at the outcome.

TipBayesian preview

Bayesian analysis compares models in two ways: by Bayes factors, which ask which model predicted the data better before seeing it, and by leave-one-out cross-validation, the Bayesian counterpart of AIC. Lesson 23 compares them, and shows why priors, not stepwise rules, are the natural way to handle many candidate predictors.

Further reading: Heinze et al. (2018) review variable selection and its pitfalls; Greenland (1989) on modeling strategy in epidemiology; Harrell (2015) on the problems of stepwise selection.

Check your understanding

Key takeaways

  • Residual patterns over time, residual autocorrelation and overdispersion show what a model is missing.
  • Nested models are compared with likelihood ratio tests; any models fitted to the same data can be compared with AIC (QAIC for quasi-Poisson). Lower is better.
  • More complex models always fit the training data better. Cross-validation estimates how well they predict new data.
  • Influential points combine high leverage with large residuals. Check them; don’t silently delete them.
  • Data-driven selection, such as stepwise regression, invalidates the p-values and intervals of the final model.
