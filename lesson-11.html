Lesson 11 · Nonlinear effects: categories, polynomials, splines and GAMs

Part II · Frequentist regression

Why categorizing a continuous exposure loses information, polynomials, regression splines, and penalized splines in generalized additive models.

About 3 hours Before this: Lessons 8 and 10 Threads: shrinkage

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • explain what is lost when a continuous exposure is cut into categories;
  • describe why polynomials behave badly at the edges of the data;
  • build a regression spline from basis functions and choose its flexibility;
  • explain a penalized spline as a flexible curve whose wiggliness is restrained by the data;
  • present a nonlinear exposure–response function relative to a reference value.

Why it matters. Temperature affects health in a U shape: cold days and hot days both bring more emergency visits. A single slope can’t describe that. How you model the curve decides what you conclude about the minimum-risk temperature, the size of heat effects, and the burden attributable to heat (Lessons 14 and 16).

Throughout this lesson the outcome is daily city-wide emergency visits, modeled by quasi-Poisson regression adjusted for season and day of week. The exposure is the average temperature over the same day and the three before it. (Lesson 16 models the lags properly.)

11.1 Categories: the step function

The simplest way to allow a nonlinear effect is to cut temperature into categories and estimate a rate ratio for each, relative to a reference category. The result is a step function: flat within each category, with jumps at arbitrary cut-points. Days just either side of a cut-point are treated as completely different; days at opposite ends of a category as identical.

Further reading: Bennette and Vickers (2012) argue against quantile categories in epidemiology; Royston et al. (2006) on the costs of dichotomizing; Greenland (1995) on alternatives to categorical dose–response analysis.

11.2 Polynomials and their wild tails

Adding T², T³ and so on lets the curve bend smoothly. But polynomials are global: every coefficient affects the whole curve, so data in the middle of the range dictate behavior at the extremes, where data are sparse. High-degree polynomials swing wildly at the edges, exactly where heat and cold effects matter most.

Further reading: Harrell (2015) on why polynomials and categories are inferior to splines; Fox (2016) on polynomial regression; Greenland (1995) on modeling dose–response trends.

11.3 Regression splines: local flexibility

A spline joins polynomial pieces smoothly at points called knots. A natural (restricted) cubic spline is also forced to be straight beyond the outer knots, which tames the tails. It enters the model as a set of basis functions: new columns in the design matrix, each multiplied by its own coefficient. The fitted curve is their weighted sum. More knots mean more flexibility.

AStandard notation

\[\log \mu_t = \alpha + f(T_t) + \gamma^\top z_t, \qquad f(T) = \sum_{k=1}^{K-1} \beta_k B_k(T)\] \[RR(T) = \exp\{f(T) - f(T_{\text{ref}})\}\]

\(B_k\) are the spline basis functions; \(z_t\) the season and day-of-week terms; \(T_{\text{ref}}\) the reference temperature.

BModel list and R

visits[t] ~ quasipoisson(mu[t])
log(mu[t]) = a + f(temp[t]) + season + dow

fit <- glm(visits ~ splines::ns(temp03, df = 3) +
             sin(2*pi*doy/365.25) +
             cos(2*pi*doy/365.25) + dow,
           family = quasipoisson, data = city_daily)

CHow it’s reported

NoteR code (optional): a spline exposure–response curve relative to its minimum 
city_daily <- visits |>
  group_by(date) |>
  summarise(visits = sum(visits), temp = first(temp_city), dow = first(dow), .groups = "drop") |>
  mutate(doy = yday(date),
         temp03 = (temp + lag(temp, 1) + lag(temp, 2) + lag(temp, 3)) / 4) |>
  filter(!is.na(temp03))

fit <- glm(visits ~ splines::ns(temp03, df = 3) + sin(2*pi*doy/365.25) + cos(2*pi*doy/365.25) + dow,
           family = quasipoisson, data = city_daily)

grid <- tibble(temp03 = seq(-5, 30, by = 0.5), doy = 180, dow = factor("Wed", levels = levels(city_daily$dow)))
grid$logmu <- predict(fit, newdata = grid)
grid |> mutate(rr = exp(logmu - min(logmu))) |>
  ggplot(aes(temp03, rr)) + geom_line() + geom_hline(yintercept = 1, linetype = 2) +
  labs(x = "Mean temperature, lags 0-3 (°C)", y = "Rate ratio vs minimum")

Further reading: Perperoglou et al. (2019) compare spline procedures in R; Harrell (2015) on restricted cubic splines and knot placement; Wood (2017) on regression splines and their basis functions.

11.4 Penalized splines and generalized additive models

Choosing the number of knots is a choice of flexibility, and choosing it by looking at the results is data dredging. A penalized spline uses many knots, then restrains wiggliness with a penalty. A small penalty gives a wiggly curve; a large one shrinks it toward a straight line. The effective degrees of freedom measure the flexibility actually used. In a GAM, the penalty is estimated from the data. Move the penalty and watch the curve and the AIC.

WarningCommon pitfall

Trying several numbers of knots, or several sets of cut-points, and reporting the one that gives the most striking result. Fix the flexibility in advance, or let a penalty chosen by a criterion such as AIC or cross-validation decide.

TipBayesian preview

A penalty that shrinks spline coefficients toward a straight line is mathematically the same as a prior that says “the curve is probably smooth”. In a Bayesian model the penalty becomes a random-walk or smoothing prior whose strength is learned from the data. Lesson 25 uses this for the temperature curve and the time trend.

Further reading: Eilers and Marx (1996) introduced P-splines; Wood (2017) is the standard book on GAMs and penalty selection; Perperoglou et al. (2019) on penalized splines in R.

Key takeaways

  • Categorizing a continuous exposure gives a step function that depends on arbitrary cut-points and wastes information.
  • Polynomials are global and unstable at the extremes of the data.
  • Regression splines are flexible locally and well behaved; natural (restricted) cubic splines are linear beyond the outer knots.
  • Penalized splines let the data choose the flexibility, measured by effective degrees of freedom.
  • Present nonlinear effects as rate ratios relative to a stated reference, with confidence bands.
