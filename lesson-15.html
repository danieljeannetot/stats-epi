Lesson 15 · Time-series regression for health data

Part IV · Time, space and clustering

Trend, seasonality and day-of-week patterns; controlling for time with splines; residual autocorrelation; and the time-stratified case-crossover design.

About 3 hours Before this: Lessons 8, 10 and 11 Threads: bias · uncertainty

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • decompose a daily health series into trend, season, day of week and residual variation;
  • control for seasonal and long-term confounding with a spline of time, and judge how much flexibility it needs;
  • check residual autocorrelation and overdispersion in a time-series model;
  • explain the time-stratified case-crossover design and why it gives the same answer as a time-series model.

Why it matters. Time-series studies compare a population with itself from day to day, so slow-changing confounders such as age structure and smoking are controlled by design. What remains are things that change with time: seasons, long-term trends, weekdays and holidays. PM2.5 is higher in winter, and so are respiratory visits. Unless the season is handled carefully, part of the seasonal rise in visits is wrongly blamed on pollution.

15.1 What’s in a daily series?

A daily count of emergency visits mixes several patterns: a slow long-term trend, an annual seasonal cycle, a weekly day-of-week cycle, holidays, and day-to-day noise that may contain the effects of temperature and pollution we want to study. A regression model separates them. Switch each component on to see its contribution.

Further reading: Bhaskaran et al. (2013) is a widely used tutorial on time-series regression in environmental epidemiology; Peng and Dominici (2008) cover the methods with R examples; Armstrong (2006) discusses temperature–mortality models.

15.2 How much to control for time?

A spline of time with a few degrees of freedom per year removes the seasons and trends. Too few, and seasonal confounding leaks into the pollution estimate. Too many, and the spline starts to absorb the short-term variation that carries the information about pollution. The usual approach is to examine how the estimate changes across a range, and to choose the flexibility in advance. Here the question is the effect of PM2.5 (average of the same and previous day) on daily visits, adjusted for temperature and day of week.

AStandard notation

\[Y_t \sim \text{quasi-Poisson}(\mu_t)\] \[\log\mu_t = \alpha + \beta\,\overline{PM}_{t,t-1} + s(t; \text{df}) + f(T_t) + \gamma^\top \mathrm{dow}_t\]

\(s(t; \text{df})\) = natural spline of time; \(f\) = spline of temperature.

BModel list and R

fit <- glm(visits ~ pm01 +
             splines::ns(t, df = 7 * 5) +
             splines::ns(temp, df = 3) + dow,
           family = quasipoisson,
           data = city_daily)
WarningCommon pitfall

Choosing the degrees of freedom for time by looking at which value gives the most striking (or the most significant) pollution estimate. Fix the choice in advance from the literature, typically 4 to 8 per year, and report the sensitivity analysis.

NoteR code (optional): sensitivity of the PM2.5 estimate to the time spline 
city_daily <- visits |>
  group_by(date) |>
  summarise(visits = sum(visits), temp = first(temp_city), pm25 = first(pm25),
            dow = first(dow), holiday = first(holiday), .groups = "drop") |>
  mutate(t = row_number(), pm01 = (pm25 + lag(pm25)) / 2 / 10) |>
  filter(!is.na(pm01))

fit_df <- function(df_per_year) {
  f <- glm(visits ~ pm01 + splines::ns(t, df = df_per_year * 5) + splines::ns(temp, df = 3) + dow,
           family = quasipoisson, data = city_daily)
  b <- coef(f)["pm01"]; s <- sqrt(vcov(f)["pm01", "pm01"])
  tibble(df_per_year, rr = exp(b), low = exp(b - 1.96 * s), high = exp(b + 1.96 * s),
         dispersion = summary(f)$dispersion)
}
map(c(2, 4, 7, 10), fit_df) |> bind_rows()

Further reading: Peng et al. (2006) study how the choice of time adjustment affects pollution estimates; Bhaskaran et al. (2013) give practical guidance on choosing degrees of freedom; Peng and Dominici (2008) on sensitivity analyses.

15.3 Residual autocorrelation and dispersion

After the time spline, residuals should look like noise. If today’s residual still predicts tomorrow’s, standard errors are too small. The autocorrelation function shows this, lag by lag. With the model from 15.2, see how the residual autocorrelation and dispersion change with the degrees of freedom.

Further reading: Bhaskaran et al. (2013) on residual checks in time-series studies; Peng and Dominici (2008) on autocorrelation and overdispersion; Fox (2016) on GLM residuals.

15.4 The time-stratified case-crossover design

A case-crossover study compares each case’s exposure on the day of the event with the same person’s exposure on nearby control days. In the time-stratified version, control days are the other days on the same weekday in the same month and year. Each stratum then acts as its own comparison, so season, trend and day of week are controlled by design. Analyzed with a conditional Poisson model, it is equivalent to a time-series model with an indicator for every stratum.

AStandard notation

\[\log\mu_t = \alpha_{s(t)} + \beta\,\overline{PM}_{t,t-1} + f(T_t)\] \[L_c(\beta) = \prod_s \prod_{t \in s}\left(\frac{e^{\beta x_t}}{\sum_{t' \in s} e^{\beta x_{t'}}}\right)^{y_t}\]

\(s(t)\) = stratum (year × month × weekday). Conditioning on each stratum’s total removes \(\alpha_s\).

BModel list and R

city_daily <- city_daily |>
  mutate(stratum = interaction(year(date),
                               month(date), dow))
glm(visits ~ pm01 + splines::ns(temp, df = 3) +
      stratum, family = quasipoisson,
    data = city_daily)
# or gnm::gnm(..., eliminate = stratum)

CHow it’s reported

TipBayesian preview

In a Bayesian time-series model, the smooth function of time can be given a random-walk prior, which says that each week’s level is close to the previous week’s. Its smoothness is then estimated from the data rather than chosen as a number of degrees of freedom. Lesson 25 uses this for the trend.

Further reading: Maclure (1991) introduced the case-crossover design; Janes et al. (2005) show why time-stratified referents avoid bias; Armstrong et al. (2014) explain the conditional Poisson model and its equivalence with case-crossover analysis.

Key takeaways

  • Daily health series contain trend, season, day-of-week and holiday patterns, which confound short-term exposures that share them.
  • A spline of time controls seasonal and long-term confounding. Its flexibility should be chosen in advance and checked in a sensitivity analysis.
  • Residual autocorrelation and overdispersion show what the model misses; quasi-Poisson standard errors account for overdispersion.
  • The time-stratified case-crossover design controls time trends by design, and is equivalent to a conditional Poisson time-series model.
