Lesson 19 · Bayesian thinking: from Bayes’ rule to a model equation

Part V · Bayesian modeling

Bayes’ rule for a parameter, conjugate updating, how the model equation builds the likelihood, prior × likelihood = posterior, posterior draws, and credible intervals.

About 3 hours Before this: Lessons 2, 5 and 8 Threads: likelihood · uncertainty · frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • apply Bayes’ rule to a parameter, and update a prior for a risk with data;
  • explain how a regression’s model equation, plus a distribution, builds the likelihood;
  • describe the prior and the posterior as distributions over the parameters of the model equation;
  • turn posterior draws back into fitted curves and answers to questions;
  • interpret a credible interval, and say when it differs from a confidence interval.

Why it matters. Bayesian analysis is often presented as a different kind of statistics. It isn’t: it uses the same model equations and the same likelihood as the models in Parts II–IV. What it adds is a prior, and a different answer: not one best estimate, but a whole distribution of plausible parameter values. This lesson connects the two views step by step.

19.1 Bayes’ rule for a risk

Lesson 2 used Bayes’ rule to update the probability of asthma after a test result. Here it updates beliefs about a parameter: the risk of asthma by age 8. Start from a prior distribution, observe some children, and get a posterior. With a beta prior and binomial data, the posterior is also a beta distribution, so the update is simple arithmetic: add the cases and non-cases to the prior’s counts.

priorlikelihood (scaled)posterior

AStandard notation

\[\pi \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a, b), \qquad d \mid \pi \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, \pi)\] \[\pi \mid d \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a + d,\ b + n - d)\]

\(a + b\) is the prior’s strength in “equivalent children”; \(a/(a+b)\) its mean.

BModel list and R

d ~ binomial(n, p)
p ~ beta(a, b)

a_post <- a + d; b_post <- b + n - d
qbeta(c(0.025, 0.5, 0.975), a_post, b_post)

CHow it’s reported

NoteR code (optional): conjugate updating and a grid posterior 
set.seed(19)
smp <- cohort |> slice_sample(n = 50)
d <- sum(smp$asthma); n <- nrow(smp)
a <- 2; b <- 18                      # prior mean 10%, worth 20 children

# exact conjugate answer
qbeta(c(0.025, 0.5, 0.975), a + d, b + n - d)
#> [1] 0.1156036 0.1971324 0.3006042
# the same posterior by brute force on a grid: prior x likelihood, rescaled
grid <- tibble(p = seq(0.001, 0.999, by = 0.001)) |>
  mutate(prior = dbeta(p, a, b), lik = dbinom(d, n, p),
         post = prior * lik / sum(prior * lik))
grid |> summarise(mean = sum(p * post),
                  low = p[which(cumsum(post) >= 0.025)[1]],
                  high = p[which(cumsum(post) >= 0.975)[1]])
#> # A tibble: 1 × 3
#>    mean   low  high
#>   <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
#> 1   0.2 0.116 0.301

Further reading: Greenland (2006) introduces Bayesian methods for epidemiologists; Gelman et al. (2013) chapter-length treatments of conjugate models; Spiegelhalter et al. (2004) on priors as “equivalent data” in health research.

19.2 The model equation builds the likelihood

Now a regression. On summer days (June to August), how do emergency visits by Lakeside’s older residents depend on temperature? The model equation gives each day’s expected count from two parameters, α and β; the Poisson distribution turns that expectation into a probability for the count that actually happened. Multiply over days and you have the likelihood of that pair (α, β). Do it for every pair and you get a likelihood map. Click anywhere on it.

Across: β, the effect per °C. Up: the expected visits at 20°C, eα. Darker = more likely. The ring marks your choice.

AStandard notation

\[y_t \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\mu_t), \qquad \log\mu_t = \alpha + \beta\,(T_t - 20)\] \[L(\alpha, \beta) = \prod_t \frac{\mu_t^{y_t} e^{-\mu_t}}{y_t!}\]

BModel list and R

visits[t] ~ poisson(mu[t])
log(mu[t]) = a + b * (temp[t] - 20)

loglik <- function(a, b)
  sum(dpois(d$visits,
            exp(a + b * (d$temp - 20)), log = TRUE))

Further reading: McElreath (2020) builds Bayesian models from the model equation upward; Pawitan (2001) on the likelihood as the shared ingredient; Held and Sabanés Bové (2020) on likelihood and Bayesian inference together.

19.3 Multiply by the prior: the posterior map

The prior is another map over the same (α, β) plane: how plausible each version of the equation seemed before the data. The posterior is the two maps multiplied point by point, then rescaled. That is all “likelihood × prior” means. Try a narrow prior centered on zero (a skeptic), then a narrow prior centered on a large effect, then add more days.

Prior

× Likelihood

= Posterior

AStandard notation

\[p(\alpha, \beta \mid y) \propto p(y \mid \alpha, \beta)\; p(\alpha)\, p(\beta)\] \[\beta \sim \mathrm{Normal}(m, s^2), \qquad \alpha \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\log 8, 1)\]

BModel list and R

visits[t] ~ poisson(mu[t])
log(mu[t]) = a + b * (temp[t] - 20)
a ~ normal(log(8), 1)
b ~ normal(0, 0.03)

brms::brm(visits ~ I(temp - 20), family = poisson,
  prior = c(prior(normal(2.08, 1), class = Intercept),
            prior(normal(0, 0.03), class = b)),
  data = d)

Further reading: Gelman et al. (2013) on posterior computation by grid and simulation; McElreath (2020) on grid approximation as a teaching device; Greenland (2006) on priors in epidemiological analysis.

19.4 Back to the equation: posterior draws

Draw points from the posterior map, as MCMC does in practice, and plug each one into the model equation. Each draw gives one curve. The bundle of curves is the Bayesian answer, expressed in the language of the model. Any question, such as the effect per °C, the expected visits on a 30°C day, or the probability that heat increases visits, is answered by computing it on every draw.

AStandard notation

\[(\alpha^{(s)}, \beta^{(s)}) \sim p(\alpha, \beta \mid y), \quad s = 1, \dots, S\] \[\text{95\% credible interval for } \beta: \ \big[\beta_{(0.025)},\ \beta_{(0.975)}\big]\]

BModel list and R

draws <- posterior::as_draws_df(fit)
quantile(draws$b_ItempM20, c(.025, .5, .975))
mean(draws$b_ItempM20 > 0)         # P(heat harmful)
mean(exp(draws$b_Intercept +
         10 * draws$b_ItempM20))    # visits at 30°C

CHow it’s reported

WarningCommon pitfall

Thinking a Bayesian model has a different equation from the frequentist one. The model equation and the likelihood are the same; the prior is the only new ingredient. With plenty of data and a weakly informative prior, the posterior median and credible interval closely match the maximum likelihood estimate and the confidence interval. The interpretation is what differs.

Further reading: Gelman et al. (2013) on summarizing posterior simulations; Kruschke (2015) on credible intervals and posterior probabilities; Bland and Altman (1998) on Bayesian and frequentist interpretations side by side.

Check your understanding

Key takeaways

  • Bayes’ rule for a parameter: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. With a beta prior and binomial data, the update just adds counts.
  • The model equation, plus a distribution for the outcome, builds the likelihood. Prior and posterior are distributions over the equation’s parameters.
  • Multiplying prior and likelihood point by point gives the posterior; it lies between them, closer to whichever is more precise.
  • Posterior draws, plugged into the model equation, give a bundle of curves; any question is answered by computing on the draws.
  • A 95% credible interval contains the parameter with 95% posterior probability, given the model, prior and data.
