Lesson 1 · Describing data and epidemiological measures

Part I · Foundations

Variable types, summaries, risks, odds, rates, comparisons between groups and age standardization.

About 2 hours Before this: Lesson 0 Threads: uncertainty (preview)

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • classify variables by type, and say what that implies for summaries and models;
  • choose between mean (SD) and median (IQR), and explain why;
  • compute and interpret risks, odds and rates, and use person-time correctly;
  • compare groups with risk differences, risk ratios and odds ratios, and know when the odds ratio misleads;
  • explain why crude rates can rank districts wrongly, and fix it with direct age standardization.

Why it matters. Before any model, an epidemiologist describes. Is a district’s emergency department busier because its people are sicker, or because they are older? Is asthma more common near busy roads? The answer starts with choosing the right measure, and a wrong choice here can mislead more than any later statistical error.

1.1 Types of variables

The type of a variable decides how you can summarize it and which model can describe it. A count variable calls for Poisson models, a binary outcome for logistic models, and a continuous variable for linear models. Classify each variable from the two course datasets, then check your answer.

VariableExample valuesYour classificationFeedback

The types: binary (two values), nominal (unordered categories), ordinal (ordered categories), count (whole numbers of events), continuous (any value on a scale), and date or time.

Further reading: Stevens (1946) introduced the classification of measurement scales; Kirkwood and Sterne (2003) and Altman (1991) cover variable types in medical data.

1.2 Distributions and summaries

A summary condenses a distribution into a few numbers, and which numbers you choose depends on its shape. The mean uses every value, so a few extreme values pull it. The median is the middle value, so it ignores how extreme the extremes are. The same goes for spread: the standard deviation reacts to outliers, the interquartile range doesn’t. Skewness measures how lopsided the distribution is.

none
meanmedianthe extra value

AStandard notation

\[\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n-1}}\] \[\text{median} = Q(0.5) \qquad \text{IQR} = Q(0.75) - Q(0.25)\]

\(Q(p)\) is the value below which a fraction \(p\) of the data lies.

BModel list and R

city_daily |>
  summarise(
    n      = n(),
    mean   = mean(visits),
    sd     = sd(visits),
    median = median(visits),
    q1     = quantile(visits, 0.25),
    q3     = quantile(visits, 0.75))

CHow it’s reported

Convention: mean (SD) for roughly symmetric data, median (IQR) for skewed data.

Further reading: Altman and Bland (1996) show how to spot skewness from published summaries; Weissgerber et al. (2015) argue for showing distributions rather than bar charts; Tukey (1977) is the classic on exploring data.

1.3 Measures of occurrence: risk, odds and rate

Three numbers describe how often a health event occurs, and they answer different questions. A risk is the proportion of people who develop the outcome over a stated period. The odds compare those who develop it with those who don’t. An incidence rate counts events per unit of person-time, which is what you need when people can have repeated events, or are observed for different lengths of time, as with emergency visits.

AStandard notation

\[R = \frac{D}{N} \qquad O = \frac{D}{N - D} = \frac{R}{1 - R} \qquad I = \frac{D}{PT}\]

\(D\) = cases or events, \(N\) = people at risk, \(PT\) = person-time.

BModel list and R

cohort |>
  summarise(
    cases = sum(asthma),
    n     = n(),
    risk  = cases / n,
    odds  = cases / (n - cases))

visits |>
  summarise(
    events = sum(visits),
    py     = sum(population) / 365.25,
    rate   = 1000 * events / py)

CHow it’s reported

Risk and odds are not the same number

Emergency visits per 1,000 person-years, by district (2020–2024)

Each resident contributes one person-day for every day they live in the district, so 5 years of a population of 1,000 gives about 5,000 person-years.

1.4 Comparing groups: difference, ratio and odds ratio

To ask whether NO₂ matters, compare children with higher and lower exposure. The risk difference says how many extra cases per 100 children. The risk ratio says how many times higher the risk is. The odds ratio compares odds instead of risks. Logistic regression (Lesson 8) produces odds ratios, which is why you’ll see them so often.

When does the odds ratio exaggerate?

Keep the true risk ratio fixed at 1.5 and change how common the outcome is in the unexposed group.

AStandard notation

\[RD = R_1 - R_0 \qquad RR = \frac{R_1}{R_0}\] \[OR = \frac{R_1/(1-R_1)}{R_0/(1-R_0)} = \frac{ad}{bc}\]

Subscript 1 = exposed (high NO₂), 0 = unexposed; \(a, b, c, d\) are the cells of the 2×2 table.

BModel list and R

cohort |>
  mutate(high = no2_modeled >= 30) |>
  summarise(r1 = mean(asthma[high]),
            r0 = mean(asthma[!high])) |>
  mutate(rd = r1 - r0,
         rr = r1 / r0,
         or = (r1 / (1 - r1)) /
              (r0 / (1 - r0)))

CHow it’s reported

How the 95% confidence interval is calculated is explained in Lesson 6.

WarningCommon pitfall

Reading an odds ratio as “times the risk”. With asthma at about 14%, the two are close. For a common outcome they diverge sharply: an OR of 2.3 may correspond to a risk only 1.5 times higher.

NoteR code (optional): the 2×2 table and its measures 
cohort |>
  mutate(high_no2 = no2_modeled >= 30) |>
  count(high_no2, asthma) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = asthma, values_from = n, names_prefix = "asthma_")
#> # A tibble: 2 × 3
#>   high_no2 asthma_0 asthma_1
#>   <lgl>       <int>    <int>
#> 1 FALSE        2415      324
#> 2 TRUE         1899      362
cohort |>
  mutate(high_no2 = no2_modeled >= 30) |>
  summarise(r1 = mean(asthma[high_no2]), r0 = mean(asthma[!high_no2])) |>
  mutate(rd = r1 - r0, rr = r1 / r0,
         or = (r1 / (1 - r1)) / (r0 / (1 - r0)))
#> # A tibble: 1 × 5
#>      r1    r0     rd    rr    or
#>   <dbl> <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
#> 1 0.160 0.118 0.0418  1.35  1.42

Further reading: Altman et al. (1998) and Zhang and Yu (1998) on odds ratios for common outcomes; Bland and Altman (2000) on the odds ratio itself; Royston et al. (2006) on why cut-offs lose information.

1.5 Crude rates mislead: age standardization

Older people visit the emergency department more often. A district with many older residents will have a high crude rate even if each age group there is no worse off than elsewhere. Direct standardization asks: what would each district’s rate be if it had the same age structure as a common standard population? Here the standard is the whole city.

Bars are sorted by the selected measure. The number on the left is the district's rank. The figure on the right is its share of residents aged 65+.

AStandard notation

\[r_{jk} = \frac{D_{jk}}{PT_{jk}} \qquad w_k = \frac{N_k^{\text{std}}}{\sum_{k'} N_{k'}^{\text{std}}}\] \[ASR_j = \sum_k w_k\, r_{jk}\]

\(j\) = district, \(k\) = age group. The crude rate weights each \(r_{jk}\) by district \(j\)’s own age structure instead of \(w_k\).

BModel list and R

std_w <- visits |>
  distinct(district, age_group, population) |>
  group_by(age_group) |>
  summarise(n = sum(population)) |>
  mutate(w = n / sum(n))

visits |>
  group_by(district, age_group) |>
  summarise(r = 1000 * sum(visits) /
              (sum(population) / 365.25)) |>
  left_join(std_w, by = "age_group") |>
  group_by(district) |>
  summarise(asr = sum(w * r))

CHow it’s reported

Always name the standard population. Standardized rates are only comparable when they use the same one.

NoteR code (optional): crude and age-standardized rates 
std_w <- visits |>
  distinct(district, age_group, population) |>
  group_by(age_group) |>
  summarise(n = sum(population), .groups = "drop") |>
  mutate(w = n / sum(n))

rates <- visits |>
  group_by(district, age_group) |>
  summarise(visits = sum(visits), py = sum(population) / 365.25, .groups = "drop") |>
  mutate(rate = 1000 * visits / py)

rates |>
  left_join(std_w, by = "age_group") |>
  group_by(district) |>
  summarise(crude = 1000 * sum(visits) / sum(py),
            asr   = sum(w * rate), .groups = "drop") |>
  arrange(desc(asr))

TipBayesian preview

Every rate and risk in this lesson is an estimate from a finite number of events. A Bayesian treats each district’s true rate as an uncertain quantity with a distribution. For a large district like Old Town, the data pin the rate down tightly. For small districts, a Bayesian hierarchical model pulls the estimate part of the way toward the city average, because a few hundred events say less than tens of thousands. You’ll see this partial pooling in Lessons 18 and 24, and the uncertainty around a single rate in Lesson 19.

WarningCommon pitfalls

Comparing crude rates across populations with different age structures. Always check whether a difference survives standardization.

Mean ± SD for skewed data. For PM2.5, the mean minus two SDs comes close to zero, which is impossible for a concentration. Report the median and IQR instead.

Calling a risk a rate. “14% of children developed asthma by age 8” is a risk (cumulative incidence). A rate needs person-time in its denominator.

Further reading: Ahmad et al. (2001) describe the WHO world standard population; Keiding and Clayton (2014) give the history and the link between standardization and causal inference; Lash et al. (2021) covers standardization methods.

Key takeaways

  • The type of a variable decides how to summarize it and, later, which model fits it.
  • Skewed data are summarized by the median and IQR. The mean and SD react strongly to extreme values.
  • Risk, odds and rate answer different questions. Rates need person-time.
  • Risk differences and risk ratios are easier to interpret than odds ratios. The OR approximates the RR only when the outcome is rare.
  • Crude rates mix the effect of age structure with the effect of place. Direct standardization removes the first.
