Lesson 9 · Confounding, effect modification and interaction

Part II · Frequentist regression

Confounding and adjustment, DAGs, colliders and mediators, effect modification, and additive versus multiplicative interaction.

About 3 hours Before this: Lesson 8 Threads: bias · frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • recognize confounding and remove it by stratification or regression;
  • draw a DAG and use it to choose an adjustment set;
  • explain why adjusting for a collider, a mediator or a consequence of the outcome causes bias;
  • assess effect modification with a test of the difference, not by comparing p-values;
  • distinguish interaction on the additive and multiplicative scales, and compute the RERI.

Why it matters. The crude association between NO₂ and asthma in our cohort is substantially larger than the true effect, because children in polluted districts also differ in other ways. Deciding what to adjust for is not a statistical question, but a causal one. Adjusting for the wrong variable can create bias where there was none.

9.1 Confounding and stratification

Children in high-traffic districts have, on average, lower socio-economic position (SES), and lower SES independently raises asthma risk. SES is a confounder: it opens a non-causal route between NO₂ and asthma. Stratification compares children within groups of similar SES; regression does the same thing more smoothly. This section uses the true NO₂ exposure, available only in the simulation, so that confounding is the only source of bias.

Further reading: Lash et al. (2021) on confounding and its control; Hernán and Robins (2020) define confounding through causal structures; Keiding and Clayton (2014) trace stratification and standardization to regression.

9.2 Using a DAG to choose what to adjust for

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) draws the assumed causal relations. Each arrow means “directly causes”. Non-causal paths from NO₂ to asthma that start with an arrow into NO₂ are backdoor paths; they create confounding unless blocked. A path is blocked by adjusting for a non-collider on it. A collider (a node where two arrows meet) blocks a path on its own, and adjusting for it opens the path. Click variables to adjust for them, and watch the paths and the estimated odds ratio.

Click a variable to adjust for it (filled = adjusted). The arrows match the model that generated the data. "Clinic visit" is a variable created for this demonstration.

AStandard notation

\[\mathrm{SES} \rightarrow \mathrm{NO_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{asthma}, \quad \mathrm{SES} \rightarrow \mathrm{asthma}\] \[\text{adjustment set } Z: \ Y^{x} \perp\!\!\!\perp X \mid Z\]

The backdoor criterion: \(Z\) blocks every backdoor path from \(X\) to \(Y\), and contains no descendant of \(X\).

BModel list and R

dag <- dagitty::dagitty("dag {
  SES -> NO2; SES -> Asthma; SES -> Smoke
  Smoke -> Asthma; Green -> NO2; Green -> Asthma
  Traffic -> NO2; Sex -> Asthma; NO2 -> Asthma
  Asthma -> Wheeze; NO2 -> Clinic; Asthma -> Clinic
}")
dagitty::adjustmentSets(dag, "NO2", "Asthma")

CHow it’s reported

The adjustment set was chosen from a DAG of assumed causal relations (Supplementary Figure S1): SES and green space. Parental smoking, sex and district traffic were not required for confounding control.

WarningCommon pitfall

Adjusting for every available variable. Adjusting for a collider (here, clinic attendance) or for a consequence of the outcome (the wheeze questionnaire) creates bias. Adjusting for a variable that only affects the exposure (traffic) costs precision for no gain.

NoteR code (optional): adjustment sets and adjusted estimates 
dag <- dagitty::dagitty("dag {
  SES -> NO2; SES -> Asthma; SES -> Smoke; Smoke -> Asthma
  Green -> NO2; Green -> Asthma; Traffic -> NO2; Sex -> Asthma
  NO2 -> Asthma; Asthma -> Wheeze; NO2 -> Clinic; Asthma -> Clinic
}")
dagitty::adjustmentSets(dag, exposure = "NO2", outcome = "Asthma")
#> { Green, SES }
fit <- glm(asthma ~ I(no2_true / 10) + ses + green, family = binomial, data = cohort)
exp(coef(fit)[2])   # close to the true OR of 1.15
#> I(no2_true/10) 
#>       1.148946

Further reading: Greenland et al. (1999) introduced causal diagrams to epidemiology; Hernán et al. (2004) explain selection bias as collider bias; Textor et al. (2016) describe the dagitty software; Westreich and Greenland (2013) on why adjustment variables’ coefficients need not be interpretable.

9.3 Effect modification: compare effects, not p-values

Heat raises emergency visits more among older people. When an effect differs across levels of another variable, that variable is an effect modifier. The right test is of the difference between the effects. A common error is to report “significant in one group, not in the other” as evidence of a difference: the non-significant group may just have less data. Choose a district and compare the age groups.

AStandard notation

\[\log\mu_{tk} = \alpha_k + \beta_k (T_t - 22) + \gamma^\top \mathrm{dow}_t\] \[z = \frac{\hat\beta_{65+} - \hat\beta_{0\text{–}14}}{\sqrt{SE_{65+}^2 + SE_{0\text{–}14}^2}}\]

BModel list and R

summer <- visits |>
  filter(month(date) %in% 6:8,
         district == "Lakeside")
glm(visits ~ age_group * I(temp - 22) + dow,
    family = quasipoisson, data = summer)

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Matthews and Altman (1996) “compare effect sizes, not P values”; Knol and VanderWeele (2012) recommend how to present effect modification; VanderWeele and Knol (2014) give a full tutorial.

9.4 Interaction on the additive and multiplicative scales

Is the harm of high NO₂ larger among children whose parents smoke? The answer depends on the scale. On the multiplicative scale (ratios), the question is whether the NO₂ risk ratio differs between groups. On the additive scale (differences), it is whether NO₂ causes more extra cases in one group. The additive scale tells you where reducing exposure prevents the most cases.

AStandard notation

\[RERI = RR_{11} - RR_{10} - RR_{01} + 1\] \[\text{multiplicative: } \frac{RR_{11}}{RR_{10}\,RR_{01}}\]

\(RR_{ab}\): risk with NO₂ level \(a\) and factor level \(b\), relative to neither.

BModel list and R

cohort |>
  mutate(no2 = no2_true >= 30) |>
  group_by(no2, parent_smoke) |>
  summarise(risk = mean(asthma))

CHow it’s reported

TipBayesian preview

Hypotheses such as “heat harms the elderly more than children, and children not at all” can be stated as constraints on several parameters and compared directly with Bayes factors. Lesson 23 does exactly this for age-group heat effects.

Further reading: VanderWeele and Knol (2014) on additive and multiplicative interaction and the RERI; Knol and VanderWeele (2012) on reporting both scales; Lash et al. (2021) on effect-measure modification.

Key takeaways

  • A confounder opens a non-causal path between exposure and outcome. Stratification or regression on it closes that path.
  • What to adjust for is decided by causal assumptions, best made explicit in a DAG.
  • Adjusting for a collider, a mediator, or a consequence of the outcome introduces bias. Adjusting for a cause of the exposure only (an instrument) costs precision.
  • Effect modification is assessed by estimating the difference between effects, not by comparing p-values.
  • Interaction depends on the scale. Report effects on both additive and multiplicative scales.
