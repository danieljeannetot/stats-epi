Lesson 23 · Bayesian hypothesis testing and model comparison

Part V · Bayesian modeling

Decisions with a region of practical equivalence, Bayes factors and Lindley’s paradox, testing constrained hypotheses, and predictive model comparison with LOO.

About 3.5 hours Before this: Lessons 6, 10 and 19–22 Threads: uncertainty · frequentist ↔︎ Bayesian

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • make decisions about effects with a posterior distribution and a region of practical equivalence;
  • compute and interpret a Bayes factor, and explain why it depends so strongly on the prior (Lindley’s paradox);
  • test substantive, constrained hypotheses, such as an ordering of age-group effects;
  • compare models by their predictive accuracy with leave-one-out cross-validation, and read its diagnostics;
  • explain when Bayes factors and LOO disagree, and which question each answers.

Why it matters. “Is there an effect?”, “which hypothesis do the data favor?” and “which model predicts better?” are different questions, and the frequentist p-value of Lesson 6 answers none of them directly. Bayesian analysis offers a tool for each, but the tools behave very differently. A Bayes factor can flip from strong support for one hypothesis to strong support for the other just by widening a prior that looked harmless. Knowing why is part of using them responsibly.

23.1 From posteriors to decisions: the region of practical equivalence

A posterior distribution already answers most questions about an effect. To decide whether an effect is negligible, define a region of practical equivalence (ROPE): effects too small to matter, here a few tenths of a percent change in visits per °C. If the 95% credible interval lies inside the ROPE, the effect is practically zero; if it lies entirely outside, it is meaningful; otherwise the data are undecided. This is the Bayesian counterpart of the equivalence test of Lesson 6.

AStandard notation

\[P(\beta \in \text{ROPE} \mid y) = P(-r < \beta < r \mid y)\]

Decision rule: equivalent if the 95% credible interval lies inside \([-r, r]\); meaningful if it lies outside; otherwise undecided.

BModel list and R

draws <- posterior::as_draws_df(fit)
b <- draws$b_heat
mean(abs(b) < 0.003)          # P(in ROPE)
quantile(b, c(.025, .975))    # compare with ROPE

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Kruschke (2018) introduces the ROPE decision rule; Kruschke (2015) on posterior decisions; Lakens (2017) on the frequentist equivalence test it parallels.

23.2 Bayes factors and Lindley’s paradox

A Bayes factor compares how well two hypotheses predicted the data before seeing them: the probability of the data under H₁ divided by that under H₀. For “no effect” (β = 0) against “some effect” (β drawn from a prior), it is easily computed as the ratio of the posterior and prior densities at zero (the Savage–Dickey ratio). The catch is that H₁’s prediction depends on its prior. A very wide prior spreads its bets over absurd effects, predicts the data poorly, and so loses to H₀, however small the p-value. This is Lindley’s paradox.

AStandard notation

\[BF_{10} = \frac{p(y \mid H_1)}{p(y \mid H_0)} = \frac{p(\beta = 0 \mid H_1)}{p(\beta = 0 \mid y, H_1)} \quad \text{(Savage–Dickey)}\] \[\text{normal case: } BF_{10} = \frac{\mathcal{N}(\hat\beta;\, 0,\, s^2 + \tau^2)}{\mathcal{N}(\hat\beta;\, 0,\, s^2)}\]

\(\hat\beta\), \(s\): estimate and standard error; \(\tau\): prior SD under H₁. Posterior odds = \(BF_{10}\) × prior odds.

BModel list and R

brms::hypothesis(fit, "heat = 0")   # Savage–Dickey
# or marginal likelihoods for any two models:
brms::bayes_factor(fit1, fit0)      # bridge sampling

CHow it’s reported

WarningCommon pitfall

Using default “vague” priors and then reporting a Bayes factor. For estimation, vague priors are often harmless; for Bayes factors, they are not neutral but decisive. A Bayes factor is only as meaningful as the prior under H₁, which should express a realistic range of effects (Lesson 20) and be reported with a sensitivity analysis.

NoteR code (optional): a Savage–Dickey Bayes factor by normal approximation 
summer65 <- visits |>
  filter(month(date) %in% 6:8, age_group == "65+") |>
  mutate(heat = temp - 22)
fit <- glm(visits ~ heat + dow + district, family = quasipoisson, data = summer65)
b <- coef(fit)["heat"]; s <- sqrt(vcov(fit)["heat", "heat"])

bf10 <- function(tau) dnorm(b, 0, sqrt(s^2 + tau^2)) / dnorm(b, 0, s)
tibble(prior_sd_pct = c(0.5, 2, 10, 100)) |>
  mutate(BF10 = bf10(prior_sd_pct / 100),
         p_value = 2 * pnorm(-abs(b / s)))
#> # A tibble: 4 × 3
#>   prior_sd_pct       BF10  p_value
#>          <dbl>      <dbl>    <dbl>
#> 1          0.5 194269956. 2.16e-11
#> 2          2   377812380. 2.16e-11
#> 3         10    87356696. 2.16e-11
#> 4        100     8788436. 2.16e-11

Further reading: Kass and Raftery (1995) is the classic review of Bayes factors; Lindley (1957) described the paradox; Wagenmakers et al. (2010) explain the Savage–Dickey method; Jeffreys (1961) developed Bayes factors and their evidence scale.

23.3 Testing substantive hypotheses

Bayes factors shine when the hypotheses are scientific statements rather than “zero vs not zero”. Here are five hypotheses about how summer heat affects the three age groups:

  • H₀: no group is affected;
  • H₌: all groups are affected equally;
  • Holder: only older people (65+) are affected;
  • Horder: the effect increases with age (elderly > adults > children);
  • Hᵤ: no constraint.

Order constraints are tested by comparing how much posterior and prior probability each hypothesis’s region gets (the encompassing prior approach); equality constraints use the Savage–Dickey ratio.

AStandard notation

\[BF_{\text{order}, u} = \frac{P(\beta_{65+} > \beta_{15\text{–}64} > \beta_{0\text{–}14} \mid y, H_u)}{P(\beta_{65+} > \beta_{15\text{–}64} > \beta_{0\text{–}14} \mid H_u)}\]

With exchangeable priors, the denominator is 1/6: each of the six orderings is equally likely before seeing the data.

BModel list and R

brms::hypothesis(fit,
  "b_old > b_adult & b_adult > b_child")
# informative hypotheses with bain:
bain::bain(est, "old > adult > child",
           Sigma = vcov_est, n = n)

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Klugkist et al. (2005) introduced encompassing priors for order-constrained hypotheses; Kass and Raftery (1995) on interpreting Bayes factors; Matthews and Altman (1996) on why effects should be compared directly.

23.4 Which model predicts better? LOO and how it differs from Bayes factors

Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO) asks how well a model predicts each observation when that observation was left out of the fit. Summed over observations, this is the expected log predictive density (ELPD). Refitting the model n times would be prohibitive; Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (PSIS) approximates it from a single set of posterior draws, and its Pareto k values warn when the approximation fails. Here four models of daily visits by older Old Town residents are compared. The slider changes only the prior on the PM2.5 coefficient in model 3, and shows how differently LOO and the Bayes factor react.

AStandard notation

\[\mathrm{elpd}_{\text{loo}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p(y_i \mid y_{-i}), \qquad p(y_i \mid y_{-i}) \approx \frac{\sum_s w_i^{(s)}\, p(y_i \mid \theta^{(s)})}{\sum_s w_i^{(s)}}\]

\(w_i^{(s)} \propto 1/p(y_i \mid \theta^{(s)})\), Pareto-smoothed. Values of \(\hat k > 0.7\) flag unreliable estimates.

BModel list and R

library(loo)
loo1 <- loo(fit1); loo2 <- loo(fit2)
loo_compare(loo1, loo2)    # elpd_diff, se_diff
plot(loo2)                  # Pareto k diagnostics

CHow it’s reported

TipTwo questions, two tools

A Bayes factor asks which model’s prior predictions matched the data, so it judges the prior as part of the model. LOO asks which model’s posterior predictions will work best for new data, so a vague prior that the data quickly overrule barely matters. Use Bayes factors when the hypotheses, including their priors, are scientific claims worth comparing; use LOO when the goal is prediction or choosing a working model. Stacking (combining models by weights that optimize LOO) is often better than choosing one.

Further reading: Vehtari et al. (2017) introduced PSIS-LOO and its diagnostics; Yao et al. (2018) on stacking predictive distributions; Gronau et al. (2017) on computing marginal likelihoods by bridge sampling.

Key takeaways

  • With a posterior in hand, decide about effects with a region of practical equivalence: inside, outside or undecided.
  • A Bayes factor compares how well two hypotheses predicted the data. It depends strongly on the prior under the alternative: wide priors penalize it (Lindley’s paradox).
  • Bayes factors are most useful for scientific hypotheses with realistic priors, such as ordered or constrained effects.
  • LOO compares models by predictive accuracy for new data; check Pareto k and the standard error of differences.
  • Bayes factors and LOO answer different questions, and can disagree.
