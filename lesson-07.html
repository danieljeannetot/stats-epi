Lesson 7 · Linear regression

Part II · Frequentist regression

Least squares, interpreting coefficients, multiple regression, residuals and assumptions, and prediction.

About 2.5 hours Before this: Part I Threads: likelihood

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • fit a straight line by least squares and explain what “least squares” minimizes;
  • interpret an intercept and a slope, and a coefficient adjusted for other variables;
  • check the assumptions of linear regression from residual plots;
  • distinguish a confidence interval for the mean from a prediction interval for a new observation.

Why it matters. Land-use regression models, which estimate the air pollution each child in Example B is exposed to, are linear regressions. So are many analyses of continuous health outcomes. And every model in the rest of the course, from logistic regression to Bayesian hierarchical models, extends the same idea: an equation for the mean, plus a statement about how observations scatter around it.

7.1 A line through the data: least squares

Children living with more green space around their home tend to have lower NO₂ exposure. A straight line describes this with two numbers: an intercept (the expected NO₂ with no green space) and a slope (the change in NO₂ per unit of green space). Each child’s vertical distance from the line is a residual. Least squares picks the line that makes the sum of squared residuals as small as possible. Try to beat it by hand.

AStandard notation

\[Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0, \sigma^2)\] \[(\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1) = \arg\min_{\beta_0, \beta_1} \sum_i \big(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\big)^2\]

BModel list and R

no2[i] ~ normal(mu[i], sigma)
mu[i]  = b0 + b1 * green[i]

fit <- lm(no2_modeled ~ green, data = cohort)
coef(fit)

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Gelman et al. (2020) introduce regression through simulation and graphs; Kirkwood and Sterne (2003) cover linear regression for medical data; Fox (2016) give a fuller treatment of least squares.

7.2 Interpreting coefficients and adjusting for other variables

With several predictors, each coefficient is the expected difference in the outcome per unit of that predictor, holding the others fixed. Adding a predictor can change the coefficients of the others: here, families in greener areas also tend to have higher socio-economic position, and the district’s traffic matters too. Add predictors and watch the green-space coefficient.

Outcome: modeled NO₂ (µg/m³), all 5,000 children. Green space is always in the model.



AStandard notation

\[E[Y_i \mid x_i] = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\mathrm{green}_i + \beta_2\,\mathrm{SES}_i + \beta_3\,\mathrm{traffic}_{j[i]}\]

\(\beta_1\) is the difference in mean NO₂ per unit of green space among children with the same SES and district traffic.

BModel list and R

fit <- lm(no2_modeled ~ green + ses + traffic,
          data = cohort |> left_join(districts,
                                     by = "district_id"))
broom::tidy(fit, conf.int = TRUE)

CHow it’s reported

WarningCommon pitfall

Interpreting every coefficient in a multivariable model as an effect estimate (the “Table 2 fallacy”). The model may be well suited to estimating one exposure’s effect, while the coefficients of the adjustment variables have quite different, often uninterpretable, meanings.

NoteR code (optional): simple and multiple linear regression 
cohort_d <- cohort |> left_join(districts |> select(district_id, traffic), by = "district_id")

fit1 <- lm(no2_modeled ~ green, data = cohort_d)
fit2 <- lm(no2_modeled ~ green + ses + traffic, data = cohort_d)

bind_rows(broom::tidy(fit1, conf.int = TRUE) |> mutate(model = "green only"),
          broom::tidy(fit2, conf.int = TRUE) |> mutate(model = "adjusted")) |>
  filter(term == "green") |>
  select(model, estimate, conf.low, conf.high)
#> # A tibble: 2 × 4
#>   model      estimate conf.low conf.high
#>   <chr>         <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>
#> 1 green only   -33.9     -34.7    -33.0 
#> 2 adjusted      -9.65    -11.0     -8.31

Further reading: Gelman et al. (2020) on interpreting coefficients; Westreich and Greenland (2013) explain the Table 2 fallacy; Harrell (2015) on building multivariable models.

7.3 Residuals and assumptions

Linear regression assumes the mean is a straight-line function of the predictors, observations are independent, the scatter around the line has the same variance everywhere (homoskedasticity), and, for small-sample inference, that the errors are normal. Residual plots check these. Pick a model and look for curves, funnels or long tails.

NoteR code (optional): residual plots 
fit2 |>
  broom::augment() |>
  ggplot(aes(.fitted, .resid)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.15) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, colour = "firebrick") +
  geom_smooth(se = FALSE) +
  labs(x = "Fitted NO₂", y = "Residual")

Further reading: Fox (2016) on regression diagnostics; Harrell (2015) on checking linearity and variance assumptions; Gelman et al. (2020) on why normality of residuals is usually the least important assumption.

7.4 Predicting the mean and predicting a child

A fitted line answers two different questions. What is the average NO₂ for children with a given amount of green space? The confidence interval for the mean answers that, and it narrows as data accumulate. What NO₂ will one particular child have? The prediction interval adds the scatter of individuals around the mean, and it never gets narrower than that scatter.

95% confidence band for the mean95% prediction band for a child

AStandard notation

\[\text{mean: } \hat y_0 \pm t\,\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat y_0)}\] \[\text{new child: } \hat y_0 \pm t\,\sqrt{\hat\sigma^2 + \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat y_0)}\]

BModel list and R

new <- tibble(green = 0.3)
predict(fit1, new, interval = "confidence")
predict(fit1, new, interval = "prediction")

CHow it’s reported

TipBayesian preview

In Bayesian regression, both questions are answered from the same posterior draws: computing the line for each draw gives the uncertainty in the mean, and simulating a child from each draw gives the posterior predictive distribution. Lesson 22 does this for Poisson and logistic models.

Further reading: Gelman et al. (2020) on predictive uncertainty; Altman (1991) and Kirkwood and Sterne (2003) on confidence versus prediction intervals.

Check your understanding

Key takeaways

  • Least squares chooses the line that minimizes the sum of squared residuals; with normal errors this is also maximum likelihood.
  • A slope is the expected difference in outcome per unit of the predictor. In a multivariable model, it is that difference holding the other predictors fixed.
  • Adding a predictor can change other coefficients a lot. Only some coefficients in a model are meant as effect estimates.
  • Residual plots reveal curvature, non-constant variance and outliers. Counts often violate linear-regression assumptions.
  • A confidence interval describes the mean; a prediction interval describes a new individual and is always wider.
