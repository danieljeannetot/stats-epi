Lesson 8 · Generalized linear models: logistic and Poisson regression

Part II · Frequentist regression

Link functions, logistic regression and odds ratios, Poisson regression with offsets, and overdispersion.

About 3 hours Before this: Lessons 3, 5 and 7 Threads: likelihood · uncertainty

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • describe a GLM as a distribution, a link function and a linear predictor;
  • fit and interpret a logistic regression, and report odds ratios per a sensible exposure unit;
  • estimate risk ratios for a common binary outcome;
  • fit a Poisson regression for rates with an offset, and interpret rate ratios;
  • detect overdispersion and correct standard errors with a quasi-Poisson model.

Why it matters. Most epidemiological outcomes are binary (asthma yes or no) or counts (visits per day). Linear regression handles neither well. Generalized linear models keep the familiar “outcome = function of predictors” structure, but let the mean be a probability or a rate, and let the variance follow the data type. The odds ratios and rate ratios in most papers come from them.

8.1 Three ingredients: distribution, link, linear predictor

A GLM has a linear predictor, η = α + βx, exactly as in linear regression. A link function connects η to the mean, so the mean stays in its allowed range: the log link keeps counts positive, the logit link keeps probabilities between 0 and 1. And a distribution describes how observations scatter around the mean. Change α and β and see the same linear predictor through three links.

identity: mean = ηlog: mean = eηlogit: mean = 1 / (1 + e−η)

AStandard notation

\[Y_i \sim \text{distribution}(\mu_i), \qquad g(\mu_i) = \eta_i = \alpha + \beta x_i\]

Linear regression: normal, \(g(\mu) = \mu\). Logistic: Bernoulli, \(g(\pi) = \log\frac{\pi}{1-\pi}\). Poisson: \(g(\mu) = \log\mu\).

BModel list and R

glm(y ~ x, family = gaussian)              # identity
glm(y ~ x, family = binomial(link = "logit"))
glm(y ~ x, family = poisson(link = "log"))

CHow it’s reported

Coefficients are reported on the scale that makes sense for the link: as differences for the identity link, odds ratios \(e^{\beta}\) for the logit link, and rate ratios \(e^{\beta}\) for the log link.

Further reading: McCullagh and Nelder (1989) is the original book-length treatment of GLMs; Agresti (2013) and Fox (2016) give accessible introductions.

8.2 Logistic regression and odds ratios

Logistic regression models the log-odds of asthma as a linear function of NO₂ and other variables. Its slope, exponentiated, is an odds ratio. Because a 1 µg/m³ change is trivial, report the OR per a meaningful unit, such as 10 µg/m³ or an interquartile range. Compare the crude and adjusted estimates, and the estimate using modeled NO₂ with the one using the true exposure, known here only because the data are simulated.

observed risk in tenths of NO₂fitted logistic curve

AStandard notation

\[Y_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi_i)\] \[\mathrm{logit}(\pi_i) = \alpha + \beta\,\frac{\mathrm{NO}_{2,i}}{10} + \gamma^\top z_i, \qquad OR_{10} = e^{\beta}\]

BModel list and R

asthma[i] ~ bernoulli(p[i])
logit(p[i]) = a + b * no2[i] / 10 + g * z[i]

fit <- glm(asthma ~ I(no2_modeled / 10) + ses +
             parent_smoke + green + sex,
           family = binomial, data = cohort)
broom::tidy(fit, exponentiate = TRUE,
            conf.int = TRUE)

CHow it’s reported

WarningCommon pitfall

Reporting an OR per 1 µg/m³ (for example 1.014) with too few decimals, so it reads as “no effect”, or presenting an adjusted OR for a common outcome as if it were a risk ratio. Choose a meaningful unit, and estimate risk ratios directly when the outcome is common.

Further reading: Hosmer et al. (2013) is the standard text on logistic regression; Zou (2004) introduced modified Poisson regression for risk ratios; Altman et al. (1998) on why odds ratios mislead for common outcomes.

8.3 Poisson regression for rates: the offset

District rates of emergency visits depend on age structure and deprivation. A Poisson regression of visit counts must account for how many person-years each count comes from. That is the role of the offset: log(person-years) enters the model with a coefficient fixed at 1, so the model describes rates, not raw counts. Each row below is one district and age group over five years. Remove the offset and see what goes wrong.


AStandard notation

\[D_{jk} \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\mu_{jk})\] \[\log \mu_{jk} = \log PT_{jk} + \alpha + \gamma_k + \beta\,\mathrm{dep}_j\]

\(j\) = district, \(k\) = age group. \(e^{\gamma_k}\) and \(e^{\beta}\) are rate ratios.

BModel list and R

D[j,k] ~ poisson(mu[j,k])
log(mu[j,k]) = log(PT[j,k]) + a + g[k] + b * dep[j]

agg <- visits |>
  group_by(district_id, age_group) |>
  summarise(D = sum(visits),
            PT = sum(population) / 365.25) |>
  left_join(districts, by = "district_id")
glm(D ~ age_group + deprivation + offset(log(PT)),
    family = poisson, data = agg)

CHow it’s reported

NoteR code (optional): rate ratios from a Poisson model with an offset 
agg <- visits |>
  group_by(district_id, age_group) |>
  summarise(D = sum(visits), PT = sum(population) / 365.25, .groups = "drop") |>
  left_join(districts, by = "district_id") |>
  mutate(age_group = factor(age_group, levels = c("0-14", "15-64", "65+"), ordered = FALSE))

fit_rate <- glm(D ~ age_group + deprivation + offset(log(PT)), family = poisson, data = agg)
broom::tidy(fit_rate, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)

Further reading: Frome and Checkoway (1985) on Poisson regression for incidence rates; Clayton and Hills (1993) on rates, person-time and log-linear models; Cameron and Trivedi (2013) on count models.

8.4 Overdispersion and quasi-Poisson standard errors

A Poisson model assumes the variance equals the mean. Daily emergency counts vary more (Lesson 3). The coefficients are still estimated correctly, but their standard errors are too small. The quasi-Poisson model estimates a dispersion parameter φ and multiplies every standard error by √φ. Here the question is the effect of PM2.5 on daily visits, adjusted for season and day of the week.

AStandard notation

\[E[Y_t] = \mu_t, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = \phi\,\mu_t\] \[\hat\phi = \frac{1}{n-p}\sum_t \frac{(y_t - \hat\mu_t)^2}{\hat\mu_t}, \qquad SE_{\text{quasi}} = \sqrt{\hat\phi}\,SE_{\text{Poisson}}\]

BModel list and R

glm(visits ~ I(pm25 / 10) + sin(2*pi*doy/365.25) +
      cos(2*pi*doy/365.25) + dow,
    family = quasipoisson, data = city_daily)

CHow it’s reported

TipBayesian preview

In a Bayesian model, overdispersion is handled by choosing a negative binomial likelihood, whose extra parameter is estimated with everything else, or by adding a random effect for each day. Posterior predictive checks (Lesson 22) reveal overdispersion the model misses.

Further reading: Ver Hoef and Boveng (2007) compare quasi-Poisson with negative binomial models; McCullagh and Nelder (1989) introduced quasi-likelihood for GLMs; Agresti (2013) discusses overdispersion in count and binary data.

Key takeaways

  • A GLM = a distribution + a link function + a linear predictor. Linear, logistic and Poisson regression are all GLMs.
  • In logistic regression, \(e^{\beta}\) is an odds ratio. Report it per a meaningful unit of exposure.
  • For common outcomes, estimate risk ratios directly (for example, Poisson regression with robust standard errors).
  • In Poisson regression for rates, log(person-time) is an offset. Without it, a model describes counts, not rates.
  • Overdispersion leaves estimates unbiased but makes Poisson standard errors too small; quasi-Poisson or negative binomial models fix this.
