Lesson 27 · Causal tools from econometrics: trials, regression, IV, RD and DiD

Part VII · Causal tools from econometrics

The five tools of Mastering ’Metrics (randomized trials, regression, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, differences-in-differences) applied to the course’s city: an air-purifier trial, the omitted-variables-bias formula, traffic as an instrument for NO₂, a heat-warning threshold and a low-emission zone.

About 5 hours Before this: Lessons 7–9, 12 and 15 Threads: bias · uncertainty

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • write a naive comparison as a causal effect plus selection bias, and explain why randomization removes the bias;
  • distinguish intention-to-treat, as-treated and per-protocol estimates in a trial with imperfect adherence, and recover the effect among compliers with an instrument;
  • use the omitted-variables-bias formula to say how much a missing confounder would change a regression estimate;
  • estimate an effect with an instrumental variable, and judge its first stage and exclusion restriction;
  • estimate the effect of a threshold-based policy with a regression discontinuity design;
  • evaluate a policy with differences-in-differences, check for parallel pre-trends, and read an event study.

Why it matters. Economists and epidemiologists ask the same causal questions with different vocabularies. Mastering ’Metrics (Angrist and Pischke 2015) distills the econometric approach into five tools, “the Furious Five”, and several of its own examples are public-health questions: health insurance, the minimum legal drinking age and mortality, and domestic violence. The tools that exploit natural experiments (instruments, thresholds and policy changes) are now standard in environmental and policy epidemiology, where randomizing an exposure is rarely possible.

27.1 The selection problem and the Furious Five

Comparing children with and without an air purifier mixes two things: the effect of the purifier, and the differences between families who buy purifiers and families who don’t. The book writes this as a single line, the same potential-outcomes logic as Lesson 12:

\[\underbrace{E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0]}_{\text{observed difference}} = \underbrace{E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 1]}_{\text{effect on the treated}} + \underbrace{E[Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 0]}_{\text{selection bias}}\]

Each of the five tools is a strategy for making the selection-bias term vanish, or for finding a comparison in which it plausibly does.

Tool How it removes selection bias What the answer depends on Epidemiological counterparts
Randomized trial Treatment assigned by chance Adherence, attrition Clinical and community trials
Regression Compare people with the same values of the controls All confounders measured and modeled well Confounder adjustment (Lessons 7–9, 12)
Instrumental variables Use a variable that shifts treatment but affects the outcome in no other way Exclusion restriction; a strong first stage Mendelian randomization; physician preference; distance
Regression discontinuity Compare units just above and just below a threshold that decides treatment Nothing else jumps at the threshold Age eligibility; policy and alert thresholds
Differences-in-differences Compare changes over time in treated and untreated groups Parallel trends without the policy Policy evaluation; natural experiments

Further reading: Angrist and Pischke (2015), chapters 1–5; Hernán and Robins (2020) for the same ideas in epidemiological language.

27.2 Randomized trials, adherence and compliers

A community trial randomizes 20,000 families to receive a portable air purifier with activated-carbon filters (\(Z_i = 1\)) or not. In the simulation, using a purifier lowers a child’s NO₂ exposure by 15 µg/m³, which lowers asthma risk according to the cohort’s true model. But not every family does what it was assigned: some offered a purifier never use it, and some better-off families not offered one buy their own. The intention-to-treat comparison (by assignment) is protected by randomization; the as-treated comparison (by actual use) is not. Dividing the intention-to-treat effect by the difference in purifier use between arms, the Wald estimator, recovers the effect among compliers: families who use a purifier if, and only if, offered one.

AStandard notation

\[\text{ITT} = E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0]\] \[\text{first stage} = E[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]\] \[\text{LATE} = \frac{\text{ITT}}{\text{first stage}}\]

BHow to read it

  • \(Z_i\): the random assignment: 1 if family \(i\) was offered a purifier.
  • \(D_i\): the treatment actually received: 1 if the family used a purifier, whatever its assignment.
  • \(\text{ITT}\): intention-to-treat: the difference in outcomes by assignment. Protected by randomization; diluted by non-adherence.
  • \(\text{first stage}\): how much more often the offered arm used a purifier: the share of compliers (minus always- and never-takers’ influence).
  • \(\text{LATE}\): the local average treatment effect: the effect among compliers, families who use a purifier only if offered one.

CModel list and R

itt   <- mean(y[z == 1]) - mean(y[z == 0])
first <- mean(d[z == 1]) - mean(d[z == 0])
itt / first                      # Wald = LATE
# with covariates and SEs:
fixest::feols(y ~ ses | d ~ z, data = trial)

DHow it’s reported

Further reading: Angrist and Pischke (2015), chapter 1, and chapter 3 on compliers; Hernán and Robins (2020) on per-protocol effects in trials.

27.3 Regression and the omitted-variables-bias formula

Regression compares children with the same values of the controls. If a confounder is left out, the omitted-variables-bias (OVB) formula says exactly how much the estimate moves: the coefficient in the short regression equals the coefficient in the long regression plus the effect of the omitted variable times its association with the exposure. The formula is exact for linear regression, used here on the asthma outcome (a linear probability model, so effects are in percentage points).

AStandard notation

\[\text{long: } Y_i = \alpha + \rho\, x_i + \gamma\, w_i + e_i, \qquad \text{short: } Y_i = \alpha^s + \rho^s x_i + e^s_i\] \[\rho^s = \rho + \gamma\,\delta_{wx}\]

\(\delta_{wx}\): the slope from regressing the omitted variable \(w\) on the exposure \(x\).

BHow to read it

  • \(\rho\): the exposure coefficient in the long regression, which includes the variable \(w\).
  • \(\rho^s\): the exposure coefficient in the short regression, which leaves \(w\) out.
  • \(\gamma\): the effect of the omitted variable \(w\) on the outcome.
  • \(w_i\): the variable left out of the short regression, such as SES.
  • \(\delta_{wx}\): the association of \(w\) with the exposure: the slope from regressing \(w\) on \(x\). Bias needs both \(\gamma\) and \(\delta_{wx}\) to be non-zero.

CModel list and R

short <- lm(asthma ~ x, data = d)
long  <- lm(asthma ~ x + ses, data = d)
aux   <- lm(ses ~ x, data = d)
coef(long)["x"] + coef(long)["ses"] * coef(aux)["x"]
# equals coef(short)["x"]
NoteR code (optional): checking the OVB formula 
d <- cohort |> mutate(x = no2_modeled / 10)
short <- lm(asthma ~ x, data = d)
long  <- lm(asthma ~ x + ses, data = d)
aux   <- lm(ses ~ x, data = d)
c(short = unname(coef(short)["x"]),
  long_plus_bias = unname(coef(long)["x"] + coef(long)["ses"] * coef(aux)["x"]))
#>          short long_plus_bias 
#>     0.02476401     0.02476401
TipHow this regression differs from the regressions elsewhere in the course

The arithmetic is the same least squares; the job is different. In the book, regression is a device for “other things equal” comparisons: one causal coefficient matters, and the controls are there only to remove selection bias. Elsewhere in the course, regression also describes (Lesson 7), models counts and risks through link functions and likelihoods (Lessons 8 and 15), predicts (Lessons 10 and 26), and feeds g-computation (Lesson 12). Three differences matter in practice:

  • Effect scale. The book uses ordinary least squares even for 0/1 outcomes (the linear probability model), so effects are risk differences in percentage points, as in this section. Epidemiology usually reports odds or rate ratios from GLMs; the linear model avoids the non-collapsibility of odds ratios (Lesson 12), but can predict risks outside 0–1.
  • What is estimated. With heterogeneous effects, regression with controls gives a weighted average of within-group effects, weighting most heavily the groups where exposure varies most. G-computation and IPW (Lesson 12) target an explicitly defined average, such as the effect in the whole population.
  • Assumptions and inference. The book justifies regression as the best linear approximation to the conditional mean and relies on robust or clustered standard errors. The course more often treats the model as a description of how the data arose, checks it, and uses flexible forms (splines, DLNMs, BART) because the shape of the exposure–response is often the scientific question.
WarningBad controls

The book’s warning applies directly to epidemiology: adjusting for a variable that is itself affected by the exposure, such as a symptom or a medication taken because of exposure, is not adjustment for confounding. It blocks part of the effect or opens a collider path (Lesson 9).

Further reading: Angrist and Pischke (2015), chapter 2; Westreich and Greenland (2013) on the Table 2 fallacy.

27.4 Instrumental variables

Modeled NO₂ is measured with error (Lesson 13), which attenuates its coefficient, and there may be confounders nobody measured. An instrument is a variable that shifts exposure but affects the outcome in no other way. Here, district traffic density: it raises NO₂ strongly, and in the simulation it affects asthma only through NO₂. Two-stage least squares (2SLS) first predicts NO₂ from traffic and the covariates, then regresses asthma on the predicted NO₂. Weaken the instrument, or let traffic affect asthma directly (through noise or stress), and watch what goes wrong.

AStandard notation

\[\text{first stage: } x_i = \pi_0 + \pi_1 z_i + \kappa^\top c_i + v_i\] \[\text{second stage: } Y_i = \alpha + \lambda\, \hat x_i + \theta^\top c_i + \varepsilon_i\] \[\lambda = \frac{\text{reduced form}}{\text{first stage}} = \frac{\rho_{zy}}{\pi_1}\]

\(z\): traffic (the instrument); \(x\): modeled NO₂ per 10 µg/m³; \(c\): covariates (SES, green space).

BHow to read it

  • \(z_i\): the instrument: district traffic density.
  • \(\pi_1\): the first-stage coefficient: how strongly the instrument moves the exposure. A weak first stage makes IV unstable.
  • \(\hat x_i\): the exposure predicted from the instrument and covariates: the part of exposure driven by traffic only.
  • \(\lambda\): the IV estimate of the exposure’s effect.
  • \(\rho_{zy}\): the reduced form: the instrument’s association with the outcome. Divided by the first stage, it gives \(\lambda\).
  • \(c_i\): covariates included in both stages.

CModel list and R

fixest::feols(asthma ~ ses + green |
              x ~ traffic, data = iv)
# or AER::ivreg(asthma ~ x + ses + green |
#               traffic + ses + green, data = iv)

DHow it’s reported

NoteR code (optional): two-stage least squares by hand 
iv <- cohort |>
  left_join(select(districts, district_id, traffic), by = "district_id") |>
  mutate(x = no2_modeled / 10)
first  <- lm(x ~ traffic + ses + green, data = iv)
iv$x_hat <- fitted(first)
second <- lm(asthma ~ x_hat + ses + green, data = iv)
c(first_stage_F = unname(summary(first)$coefficients["traffic", "t value"]^2),
  iv_estimate   = unname(coef(second)["x_hat"]),
  ols_estimate  = unname(coef(lm(asthma ~ x + ses + green, data = iv))["x"]))
#> first_stage_F   iv_estimate  ols_estimate 
#>  1.677354e+03  2.716304e-02  5.757465e-03
# Standard errors from this manual second stage are wrong; use fixest::feols or AER::ivreg,
# clustered by district, since the instrument varies only between the 12 districts.
TipInstruments in epidemiology

Mendelian randomization uses genetic variants as instruments for exposures such as alcohol or BMI (Lawlor et al. 2008). Other common instruments are physician prescribing preference, distance to a facility, and policy changes. Each must pass the same three tests: it moves the exposure (testable), it is as good as randomly assigned (partly checkable), and it affects the outcome only through the exposure (not testable; it must be argued).

Further reading: Angrist and Pischke (2015), chapter 3; Hernán and Robins (2006) and Greenland (2000) for epidemiologists.

27.5 Regression discontinuity: a heat-warning threshold

The city issues a heat–health warning whenever the day’s mean temperature reaches 23°C. In the simulation, warnings reduce emergency visits among residents aged 65+ by 8% (people stay inside, drink more, check on neighbors). Comparing warning days with other days is hopeless: warning days are hot, and heat raises visits. But days at 22.9°C and 23.1°C are nearly identical in everything except the warning. Regression discontinuity fits a line on each side of the threshold and estimates the jump at it.

AStandard notation

\[Y_t = \alpha + \rho\, D_t + \beta_1 (T_t - c) + \beta_2\, D_t (T_t - c) + e_t, \qquad |T_t - c| \le h\] \[D_t = 1\{T_t \ge c\}\]

\(Y_t\): log visits (65+) on day \(t\), after removing season, trend and weekday; \(c\) = 23°C.

BHow to read it

  • \(\rho\): the jump at the threshold: the effect of the warning, on the log scale.
  • \(D_t\): 1 if a warning was issued on day \(t\), which happens exactly when temperature reaches the threshold.
  • \((T_t - c)\): the running variable centered at the threshold: distance in °C from 23°C.
  • \(\beta_2\): lets the slope differ above the threshold, so the jump isn’t confused with a change of slope.
  • \(h\): the bandwidth: only days within \(h\) °C of the threshold are used. Narrower means less bias, more noise.

CModel list and R

rd <- filter(days, abs(temp - 23) <= h) |>
  mutate(D = temp >= 23, r = temp - 23)
lm(y ~ D * r, data = rd)           # coefficient on D
# robust bandwidth choice and inference:
rdrobust::rdrobust(days$y, days$temp, c = 23)

DHow it’s reported

Further reading: Angrist and Pischke (2015), chapter 4, including the minimum legal drinking age and mortality; Bor et al. (2014) on RD designs in epidemiology.

27.6 Differences-in-differences: a low-emission zone

In July 2022 the city introduced a low-emission zone in its two central districts, Old Town and Station. In the simulation, it reduced emergency visits there by 6%. Visits were rising citywide, so a before–after comparison in the zone mixes the policy with the trend. Differences-in-differences subtracts the change in the other ten districts, which assumes that, without the policy, the zone’s visits would have followed the same trend (parallel trends). An event study checks that assumption before the policy started.

AStandard notation

\[\log Y_{jm} = \alpha_j + \tau_m + \delta\, (\text{LEZ}_j \times \text{post}_m) + e_{jm}\] \[\hat\delta_{2\times 2} = (\bar y_{\text{LEZ, post}} - \bar y_{\text{LEZ, pre}}) - (\bar y_{\text{other, post}} - \bar y_{\text{other, pre}})\]

\(j\): district; \(m\): month; \(\alpha_j\) and \(\tau_m\): district and month fixed effects.

BHow to read it

  • \(\alpha_j\): district fixed effects: absorb everything constant about each district.
  • \(\tau_m\): month fixed effects: absorb everything affecting all districts in a month, such as season, epidemics and citywide trends.
  • \(\hat\delta_{2\times 2}\): the simple difference-in-differences: the zone’s before–after change minus the other districts’ change.
  • \(\delta\): the policy effect: the extra change in the zone’s districts after the zone started.
  • \(\text{LEZ}_j \times \text{post}_m\): 1 for zone districts in months after July 2022, 0 otherwise.

CModel list and R

fixest::feols(log(visits) ~ lez_post |
              district + month,
              cluster = ~district, data = monthly)
# event study:
fixest::feols(log(visits) ~
  i(rel_half, lez, ref = -1) | district + month,
  cluster = ~district, data = monthly)

DHow it’s reported

NoteR code (optional): a low-emission zone, simulated and evaluated 
set.seed(27)
monthly <- visits |>
  mutate(month = floor_date(date, "month")) |>
  group_by(district, month) |>
  summarise(visits = sum(visits), .groups = "drop") |>
  mutate(lez  = district %in% c("Old Town", "Station"),
         post = month >= as.Date("2022-07-01"),
         visits = if_else(lez & post, rbinom(n(), visits, 0.94), as.integer(visits)))   # true effect: -6%

did <- lm(log(visits) ~ I(lez & post) + factor(district) + factor(month), data = monthly)
c(estimate_pct = unname(100 * (exp(coef(did)[2]) - 1)))
#> estimate_pct 
#>    -5.116583
WarningStaggered adoption

When policies start at different times in different places, the classic two-way fixed-effects regression can be badly biased, because already-treated units serve as controls. Newer estimators compare each cohort of adopters only with units not yet treated (Callaway and Sant’Anna 2021).

Further reading: Angrist and Pischke (2015), chapter 5; Wing et al. (2018) on DiD for public health policy; Bernal et al. (2017) on the closely related interrupted time series.

27.7 Choosing among the tools

Question in the city Natural tool Why Main threat to check
Do air purifiers prevent asthma? Randomized trial Assignment can be randomized Non-adherence: report ITT and the complier effect
How much would a missing confounder change our estimate? OVB formula Translates assumptions into numbers The size of the omitted variable’s effects is a guess
What is the effect of NO₂ when exposure is mismeasured or confounded? Instrumental variables A source of variation unrelated to confounders Exclusion restriction; weak first stage
Do heat warnings work? Regression discontinuity Warnings switch on at a threshold Other things changing at the threshold; few days near it
Did the low-emission zone reduce visits? Differences-in-differences A policy started in some districts, not others Non-parallel trends; other changes at the same time

The placebo thresholds of 27.5 and the pre-trend checks and placebo assignments of 27.6 are negative controls (Lesson 12.5): analyses where the true effect is known to be zero, so any “effect” exposes bias. These tools complement the methods of Lessons 9, 12, 13 and 15. Regression adjustment and g-computation rely on measuring every confounder. The three natural-experiment tools trade that assumption for another, about an instrument, a threshold or a trend, which is sometimes more credible and sometimes less. Matching the design to the question is the theme of the study design lesson.

Key takeaways

  • A naive comparison equals a causal effect plus selection bias; each of the five tools is a strategy to remove the bias.
  • In trials with imperfect adherence, intention-to-treat is protected by randomization; dividing it by the first stage gives the effect among compliers.
  • The omitted-variables-bias formula turns “what if we missed a confounder?” into arithmetic.
  • An instrument must move the exposure, be as good as random, and affect the outcome only through the exposure. Weak or invalid instruments mislead.
  • Regression discontinuity compares just-above and just-below a threshold; differences-in-differences compares changes over time, assuming parallel trends.

Concepts at a glance

Ideas from this lesson that are easily confused, side by side.

Concept What it is How it differs
Selection bias (econometric sense) The part of a naive comparison due to pre-existing differences between groups. Epidemiologists call much of this confounding; “selection bias” in epidemiology usually means selection into the study.
ITT vs as-treated vs per-protocol By assignment vs by treatment received vs among adherers only. Only ITT is protected by randomization.
Compliers and LATE People treated only if assigned; the effect among them. Not the average effect in everyone: always- and never-takers are excluded.
OVB formula Short coefficient = long coefficient + (effect of omitted variable × its association with exposure). Exact for linear regression; a tool for sensitivity analysis.
Instrument A variable that moves the exposure and affects the outcome only through it. Relevance can be checked; the exclusion restriction can’t.
First stage vs reduced form Instrument → exposure vs instrument → outcome. IV estimate = reduced form ÷ first stage.
Regression discontinuity Comparing units just above and below a threshold that decides treatment. Sharp: treatment jumps from 0 to 1; fuzzy: the probability of treatment jumps.
Bandwidth How far from the threshold data are used. Narrow: less bias, more noise; wide: the reverse.
Differences-in-differences The treated group’s change minus the control group’s change. Removes stable differences between groups and shared trends; assumes parallel trends.
Event study DiD effects estimated period by period around the policy start. Pre-period coefficients test parallel trends.
Randomization inference Comparing the estimate with those from placebo assignments. Valid with few treated units, where cluster-robust SEs fail.
Regression in the book vs in the course One causal coefficient, controls only to remove bias, linear even for binary outcomes vs GLMs, likelihood and flexible shapes. Same least squares; different goals, effect scales and inference conventions.
Placebo test An analysis where the true effect is known to be zero, such as a fake threshold or a fake policy. A negative control for quasi-experiments (Lesson 12.5).
