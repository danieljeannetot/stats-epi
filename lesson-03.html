Lesson 3 · Random variables and probability distributions

Part I · Foundations

Expectation and variance; the binomial, Poisson and normal distributions; densities; overdispersion and the negative binomial.

About 2.5 hours Before this: Lessons 1–2 Threads: likelihood

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • describe a random variable by its distribution, expected value and variance;
  • recognize when the binomial, Poisson and normal distributions are reasonable models;
  • explain why a probability density can exceed 1, and why only areas are probabilities;
  • diagnose overdispersion in count data, and say what the negative binomial distribution adds.

Why it matters. Every statistical model in this course is “an equation plus a distribution”. Choosing the distribution is choosing what kind of randomness you believe the data contain. Emergency visits are counts, so the Poisson distribution is the natural starting point. But real counts vary more than Poisson allows, and ignoring that makes every confidence interval too narrow.

3.1 Random variables, expected value and variance

Tomorrow’s number of emergency visits by older Lakeside residents is a random variable: before the day happens, it could take many values, each with some probability. The distribution of past days estimates those probabilities. Its center is the expected value, and its spread the variance. Choose a district and age group.

AStandard notation

\[E[Y] = \sum_{y} y \, P(Y = y)\] \[\mathrm{Var}(Y) = E\big[(Y - E[Y])^2\big]\]

\(Y\) = visits on a day; \(P(Y = y)\) is estimated by the share of days with exactly \(y\) visits.

BModel list and R

visits |>
  filter(district == "Lakeside",
         age_group == "65+") |>
  summarise(mean = mean(visits),
            var  = var(visits),
            ratio = var / mean)

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Blitzstein and Hwang (2019) and Casella and Berger (2002) define random variables, expectation and variance; Clayton and Hills (1993) introduce them in an epidemiological setting.

3.2 The binomial and Poisson distributions

The binomial distribution counts successes in a fixed number of independent trials, such as the number of children with asthma among 30 in a class. The Poisson distribution counts events that occur independently at a constant rate, such as emergency visits per day. When there are many trials, each with a small probability, the binomial becomes the Poisson. Move the sliders, and try “binomial with large n” to watch it happen.

AStandard notation

\[P(Y = k) = \binom{n}{k}\pi^k (1-\pi)^{n-k}\] \[P(Y = k) = \frac{\mu^k e^{-\mu}}{k!}\]

Binomial: \(E[Y] = n\pi\), \(\mathrm{Var}(Y) = n\pi(1-\pi)\). Poisson: \(E[Y] = \mathrm{Var}(Y) = \mu\).

BModel list and R

# a class of 30 children
y ~ binomial(n = 30, p = 0.13)
dbinom(0:10, size = 30, prob = 0.13)

# visits on a day
y ~ poisson(mu = 8)
dpois(0:20, lambda = 8)

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Blitzstein and Hwang (2019) derive the Poisson as a limit of the binomial; Clayton and Hills (1993) use both as the basis of epidemiological models; Kirkwood and Sterne (2003) give the practical rules for when each applies.

3.3 The normal distribution, and why a density is not a probability

Continuous variables, like temperature or NO₂, are described by a probability density. The probability of falling in an interval is the area under the curve over that interval. A density’s height is not a probability: when the distribution is narrow, the curve must be tall to keep the total area at 1, so its height can exceed 1. Change the mean, spread and interval.

AStandard notation

\[f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\] \[P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\]

BModel list and R

x ~ normal(mu, sigma)

dnorm(0, mean = 0, sd = 0.2)  # height: 1.99
pnorm(1.96) - pnorm(-1.96)    # area: 0.95

CHow it’s reported

Further reading: Altman and Bland (1995) on the normal distribution in medical statistics; Casella and Berger (2002) on densities and distribution functions; Blitzstein and Hwang (2019) on continuous random variables.

3.4 Overdispersion and the negative binomial

A Poisson model insists that the variance equals the mean. Real emergency counts vary more, because days differ in ways the model doesn’t know about: winter, Mondays, heat waves. This extra variation is overdispersion. The negative binomial distribution allows it by letting the rate itself vary from day to day. Compare the observed counts with both models, then remove sources of variation and watch the overdispersion shrink.

observed daysPoisson, same meannegative binomial, same mean and variance

AStandard notation

\[Y \sim \mathrm{NegBin}(\mu, \phi)\] \[E[Y] = \mu, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(Y) = \mu + \frac{\mu^2}{\phi}\]

As \(\phi \to \infty\) the negative binomial becomes Poisson. Method-of-moments estimate: \(\hat\phi = \bar y^2 / (s^2 - \bar y)\).

BModel list and R

y ~ neg_binomial_2(mu, phi)

d <- visits |> filter(district == "Old Town",
                      age_group == "65+")
m <- mean(d$visits); v <- var(d$visits)
phi_hat <- m^2 / (v - m)
dnbinom(0:60, mu = m, size = phi_hat)

CHow it’s reported

WarningCommon pitfall

Assuming counts are Poisson without checking. If the variance is three times the mean, Poisson standard errors are about √3 ≈ 1.7 times too small, and a “significant” result may be nothing of the kind. Lessons 8 and 15 handle overdispersion inside regression models.

NoteR code (optional): checking for overdispersion 
visits |>
  group_by(district, age_group) |>
  summarise(mean = mean(visits), var = var(visits),
            ratio = var / mean, .groups = "drop") |>
  arrange(desc(ratio)) |>
  head()

TipBayesian preview

The negative binomial is a Poisson whose rate varies at random from day to day according to a gamma distribution. In Bayesian language, that is a hierarchical model: each day has its own rate, drawn from a common distribution. The same idea, with districts or schools instead of days, gives the hierarchical models of Lesson 24.

Key takeaways

  • A random variable is described by its distribution; its expected value is the long-run average and its variance the spread.
  • The binomial counts successes in n trials; the Poisson counts events at a constant rate, and has variance equal to its mean.
  • For continuous variables, probabilities are areas under a density. A density’s height can exceed 1.
  • Real counts are usually overdispersed. Check the variance-to-mean ratio, and use negative binomial or quasi-Poisson models when it is well above 1.
