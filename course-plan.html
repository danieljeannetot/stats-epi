Statistics for epidemiology: from probability to Bayesian hierarchical models

Course plan, version 2. Twenty-five interactive lessons built around two running examples from environmental epidemiology: heat and emergency visits in a simulated city, and traffic-related air pollution and childhood asthma in the same city. The course starts with probability and finishes with Bayesian spatio-temporal hierarchical models.

1 · Approach and principles

The course teaches statistics the way the earlier walkthroughs did: by letting students change something and watch what happens, with a running narration that explains each change. Every idea is shown on the same epidemiological problem, so students never have to learn a new context and a new method at once. Mathematics is kept to what is needed to read a model equation and understand where an answer comes from.

Frequentist methods are taught first and taught properly, because most of the literature students will read uses them. The Bayesian part then revisits the same models, so that students see one model through two lenses rather than two separate subjects. Parts I to IV include short "Bayesian preview" boxes. From Lesson 19 onward, lessons show both approaches side by side, in the same two-column format as the earlier pages.

R code is optional throughout. Each concept can be followed fully without it. Code appears in collapsed panels, written in tidyverse style, for students who want to reproduce the analysis on the course dataset.

2 · The two running examples and their data

Both examples are set in the same simulated city with 12 districts, so students can see how one setting produces very different study designs and data structures. Because the data are simulated, every lesson can reveal the true values, so students can check whether a method recovers the truth. That's the most powerful teaching device in the earlier pages.

Example A · Heat and emergency visits (time series, counts)

Five years of daily data, aggregated by district and age group. This is the example from the earlier pages, and it carries the count models, time series, lagged effects, spatial models and the capstone.

Example B · Traffic-related air pollution and childhood asthma (cohort, binary outcome)

A birth cohort of about 5,000 children living in the same 12 districts, followed to age 8. This individual-level example carries the binary-outcome models, causal inference, measurement error and disease burden lessons. It suits them well: air pollution exposure is always estimated rather than measured, confounding by socio-economic position is the central threat, and the burden of asthma attributable to NO₂ is a standard policy question.

The browser generates both datasets from fixed seeds, so every student sees the same numbers. Lesson 0 also provides an R script (tidyverse) that generates the identical data, so code snippets run without downloads. The true data-generating models contain things students discover gradually: a U-shaped temperature effect with lags, stronger heat effects in the 65+ group, overdispersion, spatially correlated district effects, confounding of NO₂ by socio-economic position, and exposure error that biases naive estimates toward the null.

3 · What every lesson contains

Header

Lesson number, part, prerequisites, estimated time, and three to five learning objectives written as things the student will be able to do.

Why it matters

A short epidemiological motivation: the question from the running example that this lesson answers, and what goes wrong without the method.

Concept sections

Two to four sections, each with an interactive demonstration and a "What's happening" box that narrates every change the student makes.

Optional R code

Collapsed panels with tidyverse code that reproduces the demonstration on the course data. Closed by default, with a copy button.

Bayesian preview

In Parts I–IV, a short box showing how a Bayesian would see the same idea, and pointing to the lesson where it is developed. From Part V, full side-by-side columns.

Common pitfalls

Two or three misinterpretations that appear in published papers, such as reading a p-value as the probability the null is true.

Check your understanding

Five to eight concept questions mixing statistical and epidemiological reasoning, such as "which DAG justifies this adjustment?" or "what does this interval mean?". Instant feedback explains why each answer is right or wrong. Some ask students to predict what a demo will do before running it. No applied exercises.

Key takeaways and glossary

A short summary, links to glossary terms used in the lesson, and links to the previous and next lessons.

4 · Five threads through the course

Five ideas recur across many lessons. Each lesson card below is tagged with the threads it advances, so the course has a visible spine rather than being a list of techniques.

Likelihood

From "how probable is this count?" in Lesson 3 to the full likelihood of a hierarchical model. Every model is "an equation plus a distribution".

Uncertainty

Sampling variation, standard errors, confidence and credible intervals, and propagating uncertainty into derived quantities like the MMT or attributable fractions.

Shrinkage

Penalized splines, mixed models, priors and hierarchical models are all ways of borrowing strength. This thread prepares the course's ending.

Bias

Confounding, selection, misclassification and exposure measurement error: the ways an estimate can be precise and still wrong. Introduced with DAGs in Lesson 9, developed in Part III.

Frequentist ↔ Bayesian

Where the two approaches agree, where they differ, and what each assumes. Previewed in every lesson of Parts I–IV and developed fully in Part V.

Part III

Causal inference, measurement and disease burden

Three topics that decide whether an estimate means what we want it to mean, and what it implies for population health. Mostly Example B.

12Causal inference: from association to effect3.5 h
Objectives
State a causal question as a contrast of potential outcomes. Explain exchangeability, positivity and consistency. Estimate an average causal effect with g-computation, propensity scores and inverse probability weighting. Assess sensitivity to unmeasured confounding.
Key concepts
Potential outcomes and counterfactuals, identification assumptions, the target trial framework, standardization and g-computation, propensity scores (matching, weighting), positivity violations, marginal vs conditional effects, non-collapsibility of the odds ratio, E-values and quantitative bias analysis.
Interactive
A "two worlds" view of the cohort: every child's asthma outcome under high and low NO₂, with only one ever observed. A propensity-score overlap plot where students tighten exposure contrasts until positivity breaks. A comparison of the conditional OR, marginal OR and risk difference from the same data. An E-value slider for an unmeasured confounder.
R (optional)
G-computation with glm() and mutate() on counterfactual copies of the data, WeightIt or manual IPW, cobalt for balance, EValue.
Pitfall
Interpreting an adjusted odds ratio as a population effect. Adjusting for everything available, including mediators.

13Measurement error and misclassification3 h
Objectives
Distinguish classical from Berkson error and non-differential from differential misclassification. Predict the direction of bias each causes. Correct an estimate using validation data with regression calibration or simulation.
Key concepts
Classical vs Berkson error, regression dilution, error in confounders, misclassification of binary outcomes and exposures, sensitivity and specificity as error parameters, validation and reliability studies, regression calibration, SIMEX, probabilistic bias analysis.
Interactive
Modeled vs personal NO₂ in the validation subsample, with an error slider that flattens the exposure–response slope (classical) or leaves it unbiased but noisier (Berkson). 6 · Technical design

Each lesson is a single, self-contained HTML file that works offline in any modern browser, like the pages you've already used. All lessons share one design system (colors, typography, light and dark mode), so the course feels like one product. A home page, index.html, links the lessons and shows progress.

ComponentDesign
File structureOne folder: index.html, lesson-00.html to lesson-25.html, glossary.html, and course-data.R (both datasets). Relative links between files, so the folder can be zipped, emailed or put on a course website or Moodle.
InteractivesPlain JavaScript and SVG or canvas, with no external libraries. The browser does real computation: likelihoods, Newton fits, MCMC, simulations.
Optional R codeCollapsed panels (closed by default) with tidyverse-style code and a copy button. Packages named in each panel. A shared setup script in Lesson 0.
QuizzesMultiple choice and "predict, then reveal" questions with immediate explanations. Input is checked before feedback is shown. No grading or server.
Progress"Mark as complete" stored in the browser, so the home page shows which lessons are done. Kept on that device only.
AccessibilityKeyboard-operable controls, text descriptions for plots, sufficient contrast in both color modes, reduced-motion support, printable layout.
GlossaryOne page of definitions, linked from terms in each lesson.

Notation: all three forms, side by side

Every model and measure is shown in three forms at once, in a three-column panel that stacks on narrow screens. A, standard notation, comes first, because students need to read papers and textbooks. B, the model list with R, sits right next to it, so the translation from symbols to code is line by line. C, epidemiological reporting, is always present, showing how the result would appear in a results section. Lessons 0 and 1 already use this panel.

FormExample
A · Standard notationYᵢ ~ Bernoulli(πᵢ), logit(πᵢ) = α + β·(NO₂ᵢ − 25)/10 + …
B · Model list and Rasthma ~ bernoulli(p) / logit(p) = alpha + beta * (no2 - 25) / 10 + …, then the glm(), glmer() or brm() call
C · Epidemiological reporting"OR 1.15 per 10 µg/m³ (95% CI …), adjusted for …"

Fixed conventions, kept in the symbols table on the glossary page:

Symbol or conventionMeaning
αIntercept or baseline (log or logit scale)
βExposure effects; β_ℓ for lag ℓ
γConfounder and adjustment terms
u_j, σ, τGroup effects for district or school j; residual and between-group standard deviations
Indicesi child, t day, j district or school, k age group, ℓ lag
Effect scalesHeat: % change per °C and RR relative to the MMT. NO₂: OR and RR per 10 µg/m³. Burden: attributable cases, PAF and DALYs.
IntervalsAlways named in full: 95% confidence interval or 95% credible interval
